A carbon price that stops at the border

Aviation is covered by the EU ETS, but only partly, and the reason is diplomatic rather than technical. When the sector was brought in from 2012 the rules covered every flight arriving at or departing from a European airport, including long-haul. Third countries objected forcefully that Europe was pricing emissions occurring in their airspace and over the high seas. Facing a trade dispute, the EU suspended the extra-European part of the scope — the measure known as "stop the clock" — pending a global scheme through the International Civil Aviation Organization.

That suspension has been extended repeatedly and is still in place. The result is a system that prices a Berlin–Madrid flight in full and a Berlin–Singapore flight not at all.

Covered — flights between airports in the European Economic Area, plus departures to the United Kingdom and Switzerland
Not covered by the ETS — flights departing the EEA for anywhere else, however long
Those departing flights are instead subject to CORSIA, a fundamentally weaker instrument — see the CORSIA tab.

The 2023 revision extended the geographic restriction to the start of 2027. From that point, departing flights to states not implementing CORSIA would come back into the ETS — a deliberate lever to push third countries into the global scheme rather than a settled policy.

Why this matters more than it sounds

The excluded flights are the emitting ones

Long-haul flights burn far more fuel per passenger than short hops, and it is precisely long-haul that sits outside the ETS. So the sector's coverage figure — around 58 Mt priced in 2025 — is not a measure of aviation's climate impact but of the portion Europe felt able to price without provoking a trade fight.

And CO₂ is only a third of the problem

Contrails, nitrogen oxides and water vapour at altitude do more warming than the CO₂ does. The EU's own analysis puts non-CO₂ effects at 66% of aviation's net climate forcing. None of it is priced anywhere in the world. Monitoring began in 2025; that is all.

Two exclusions compounding. Roughly speaking, the ETS prices the CO₂ from the shorter flights. Put the geographic restriction and the non-CO₂ gap together and the priced share of aviation's actual contribution to warming is a small fraction of the whole — which is the single most important thing to understand about this sector.

Aviation has its own allowances, and its own trajectory

Aviation allowances are a separate quantity from the main ETS cap for stationary installations, calculated bottom-up rather than from the reduction factor applied to industry. Aircraft operators may use general allowances to comply, but the allowances issued for aviation are their own pool. From 2024 that pool shrinks by a linear reduction factor of 4.3% a year.

This is worth holding onto when reading the charts on the ETS overview page: the headline cap figure quoted there is for electricity, heat, industry and maritime. Aviation sits alongside it, not inside it.

The end of free allocation

Airlines received most of their allowances free of charge from 2012. The 2023 revision ended that on a three-year timetable, and 2026 is the first year in which the sector buys every allowance it needs. Aviation is therefore the first ETS sector to reach full auctioning — ahead of industry, which is still being weaned off free allocation through the CBAM phase-out to 2034.

The 2024 and 2025 allocations were shared out in proportion to each operator's verified 2023 emissions — a backward-looking distribution key that rewards the airlines that flew most in the recovery year, and one that no longer matters from 2026 because there is nothing left to distribute.

What this actually costs. Free allocation was worth little when allowances traded at €5 and a great deal at €75. Ending it does not change the number of tonnes the sector may emit — the allowances still exist and are still sold — but it moves the entire carbon bill onto the operator's income statement in three years.

Verified emissions, 2012–2025

The clearest shock-and-recovery series anywhere in the ETS. Aviation entered in 2012 at 74.4 Mt under the original full geographic scope; the drop to 47.4 Mt in 2013 is not abatement but the "stop the clock" narrowing of coverage. From there, steady growth to 2019, collapse in 2020, and a recovery that is now essentially complete.

What the shape tells you

2020 was not a policy success

Emissions fell 63% in a single year, from 67.1 Mt to 24.7 Mt. Nothing about the instrument caused that, and nothing about it persisted. It is a useful reminder when reading any emissions chart covering 2020: the carbon price did not do this.

The recovery is the real result

By 2024 emissions were back to 61.5 Mt, easing to 58.2 Mt in 2025 — about 13% below the 2019 peak, measured on the same restricted scope. Aviation is the only ETS activity whose low point is behind it rather than at the end of the series. Every other sector's chart trends down.

A carbon price of €75 a tonne adds roughly €3 to the cost of a short-haul seat. That is not nothing across an airline's fleet, but it is small against fuel, crew and airport charges — which is the honest explanation for why a decade of carbon pricing has not bent this line.

What CORSIA is

The Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation is the global measure agreed at ICAO in 2016 and the reason the EU suspended its extra-European scope. It is not a carbon market and not a cap. It requires airlines to offset growth in emissions above a 2020 baseline by buying credits from projects elsewhere — leaving the baseline itself entirely unpriced.

EEA countries implement CORSIA for their own airlines through the ETS Directive itself, so for a European carrier the two schemes are administered as one system with two different rulebooks depending on where the aircraft is going.

The two instruments are not comparable

EU ETS — a cap. Every tonne needs an allowance. Allowances are finite and shrinking.
CORSIA — an offsetting obligation. Only growth above 2020 is covered, and it is met by buying credits, not by reducing.

The practical difference is the price. An EU allowance has traded around €75; eligible CORSIA credits have generally been available for a small fraction of that. A tonne of CO₂ from a flight to Madrid and a tonne from a flight to Singapore therefore carry entirely different costs, for reasons that have nothing to do with the atmosphere.

The Commission is required to assess whether CORSIA is delivering genuine climate benefit, and that assessment governs what happens to the geographic restriction from 2027. If CORSIA is judged inadequate, departing flights to non-participating states return to the ETS. This is the live question in the sector, and it is not yet resolved.

The two thirds nobody prices

Burning kerosene at altitude does more than release CO₂. Contrails and the cirrus cloud they seed trap outgoing heat; nitrogen oxides alter ozone and methane concentrations; water vapour is injected directly into the upper troposphere. Taken together, the European Aviation Safety Agency assessed these non-CO₂ effects at 66% of aviation's net climate forcing in 2018 — twice the contribution of the CO₂ that the ETS actually charges for.

Nothing in the world prices any of this. Not the ETS, not CORSIA, not any national scheme. The entire regulatory response so far is a measurement exercise.

That is less feeble than it sounds. Contrail formation depends on atmospheric conditions along a specific flight path at a specific moment, so the warming caused by two identical aircraft flying the same route on different days can differ by orders of magnitude. Any pricing instrument needs a per-flight measurement method that did not exist.

The monitoring framework

What operators must do

From 1 January 2025, aircraft operators monitor the non-CO₂ effects of their flights and report annually. The method computes a CO₂-equivalent figure per flight from flight trajectory, aircraft and engine characteristics, fuel properties and meteorological data supplied by national weather services. Coverage is phased, beginning with flights involving an EEA airport.

What happens next

By the end of 2027 the Commission must report on the results and, if appropriate, propose legislation. That is the decision point at which non-CO₂ effects either enter a pricing instrument or do not. Until then the obligation creates data and nothing else.

It is worth being clear about the sequencing, because it is unusual. The EU has legislated a measurement system for an effect it has not decided how to regulate, on the reasoning that the science cannot mature without the data. Whether that is prudence or delay is a fair question, and both answers are argued seriously.

Two instruments pushing the same fuel

The carbon price makes flying more expensive but does little to make sustainable aviation fuel available, because SAF costs several times what fossil kerosene does and no airline can buy what refiners do not make. Two separate measures address that directly.

ReFuelEU Aviation — the mandate

Regulation (EU) 2023/2405 obliges fuel suppliers at EU airports handling at least 800 000 passengers or 100 000 tonnes of freight a year to blend a rising share of SAF: 2% from 2025, 6% by 2030, reaching 70% by 2050. A separate sub-mandate for synthetic e-fuels starts at 0.7% in 2030 and climbs to 35% by 2050. This is a supply-side obligation: it binds the fuel supplier, not the airline.

The ETS SAF reserve — the subsidy

20 million allowances — about €1.6 bn at €80 a tonne — are set aside from 2024 to 2030 to cover part or all of the price gap between SAF and fossil kerosene for fuel actually uplifted. Support reaches 100% of the difference for fuel uplifted at small airports, small islands and outermost regions, where the cost penalty would otherwise be prohibitive.

The design is deliberate: a mandate to create demand, a subsidy to blunt the cost, and a carbon price to make the alternative less attractive. It is also a rare case of ETS revenue being recycled straight back into the sector that generated it — the same criticism levelled at indirect cost compensation applies here, and the same defence is offered.

Aviation has the worst compliance rate in the ETS

For the 2024 compliance year the Union Registry assigns each operator a code: A if it surrendered at least as many allowances as it emitted, B if it did not. Aircraft operators come in at 91.2% — 436 compliant against 42 that were not — the lowest of any activity in the system, where the long-established stationary sectors sit between 98% and 100%.

Two very different figures, both correct. The Commission reports that over 99.5% of aviation emissions are covered by surrendered allowances. The 91.2% here counts operators. Both are true: the non-compliant operators are overwhelmingly small ones — business jets, charter operators, occasional visitors to EEA airspace — and between them they account for a negligible share of the tonnes. Which number is the right one depends entirely on whether you are asking about environmental integrity or about administrative reach.

The pattern across the system is that compliance tracks how long a sector has been regulated and how concentrated it is, rather than how hard its abatement is. Cement clinker and refining record no failures at all. Aviation, with hundreds of small operators administered across 30 states, records the most.

Why the 2012 figure is not comparable with the rest

2012 is the only year in the series measured under the original full geographic scope, covering flights to and from third countries. The fall to 2013 is a change in what was counted, not a change in what was emitted. Any percentage change computed from a 2012 baseline will overstate the reduction substantially — which is why the comparisons on this page use 2013 onwards, or state the scope change explicitly.

What this page does not cover

Not covered here: the detailed rules on small-emitter exemptions and the de minimis thresholds below which an operator falls out of scope; the treatment of flights to the outermost regions; the interaction with national aviation taxes, which several member states levy alongside the ETS; and the aviation provisions of the July 2026 ETS revision proposal, which is in negotiation and not law.

On the €3 a seat figure

An order-of-magnitude illustration, not a computed fare impact. A short-haul flight emits roughly 80–130 kg CO₂ per passenger depending on aircraft, load factor and distance; at €75 a tonne that is €6–10 of carbon cost per seat, of which airlines historically passed on part while receiving the rest of their allowances free. With free allocation ending in 2026 the full amount is now a real cost. Treat the figure as indicating scale rather than as a price.
