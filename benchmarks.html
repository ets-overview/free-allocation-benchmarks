All 54 benchmark values across the three allocation periods — 2013–2020 (Decision 2011/278/EU), 2021–2025 (Reg. (EU) 2021/447) and 2026–2030 (Reg. (EU) 2026/1412) — with the derivation of each value, the CBAM phase-out overlay, and an installation-level allocation calculator.

What a benchmark is

A benchmark is a rate: tonnes of CO₂e allowed per tonne of product. It is not a target and not a cap. It is the yardstick used to decide how many free allowances an installation receives — and it is set by what the cleanest producers in Europe actually achieve, not by what is comfortable for the average one.

An installation making 1 Mt of grey cement clinker in 2026 receives
1 000 000 t × 0.656 = 656 000 allowances, free of charge

If it emits less than that, it keeps the surplus and can sell it. If it emits more, it buys the difference. The benchmark is therefore a reward for being better than the reference and a bill for being worse — the same rate applied to everyone, regardless of how they actually produce.

Where the numbers come from

The cleanest 10%

Each benchmark starts from the average greenhouse gas intensity of the 10% most efficient installations producing that good in the EU, measured over two reference years. That deliberately places most installations above the line: the majority are supposed to be short, or the instrument would not push anyone.

54 of them

52 product benchmarks, each covering a defined good with its own system boundary, plus two fall-backs — a heat benchmark and a fuel benchmark — used where no product benchmark fits. Everything from hot metal and cement clinker to adipic acid and tissue paper has its own value.

They are updated twice a decade, not annually. Values are fixed for 2013–2020, then 2021–2025, then 2026–2030. Within a period nothing moves. That is why the charts on this page show three steps rather than a curve — and why a benchmark update is a significant event for the sectors it covers rather than a routine adjustment.

Benchmark value vs. what the cleanest 10% actually achieved

Each point is one benchmark. The horizontal axis is the value in force; the vertical axis is what the cleanest 10% of installations actually achieved in 2021–2022. If the two matched, every point would sit on the diagonal. Points below the line are benchmarks set more generously than measured best practice — the gap is the statutory 6%–50% bounds on the total reduction doing the work instead of the data. Both axes are logarithmic because the values span three orders of magnitude, from 0.009 for dried gypsum to 7.98 for hydrogen.

The further a point sits below the diagonal, the less the benchmark reflects what is actually achievable. Nitric acid is the extreme case: the cleanest installations manage 0.015, while the benchmark is set at 0.151 — ten times more generous, because the 50% cap on the total reduction stopped the value falling any further.

Trajectory, indexed to 2013–2020 = 100

The same benchmarks rebased so that 2013–2020 equals 100, which makes trajectories comparable across goods measured in completely different units. Values are fixed within each period, so these are step changes rather than trends — the flat segments are real. Pick a few benchmarks to compare; the default selection spans the range, from hot metal, which has barely moved, to newsprint, which has halved twice.

How each 2026–2030 value is actually derived

Article 10a(2) does not simply adopt observed best practice. It computes an annual reduction rate by comparing the measured 2021/22 average of the 10% most efficient installations against the original 2007/08-based benchmark over 14 years, extrapolates that rate linearly over 20 years (2007/08 → 2027/28), then clamps the total reduction to between 6% and 50%. The clamp binds for a large share of benchmarks, which means their values are set by the statutory limit rather than by data.

The CBAM factor: nominal vs. effective allocation

For goods listed in Annex I to the CBAM Regulation (EU) 2023/956, free allocation is multiplied by an annual CBAM factor that falls to zero in 2034. A benchmark that looks stable across 2026–2030 therefore delivers a rapidly shrinking allocation in practice: the effective value halves between 2028 and 2030 alone. Non-CBAM carbon-leakage sectors keep a factor of 100% with no phase-out date set.

The dashed scenario is not law. The Commission's ETS review proposal of 17 July 2026 (COM(2026) 619) would reintroduce 15% of the phased-out free allocation from 2028 and extend the phase-out to 2038. Modelled here as factor′ = factor + 0.15 × (1 − factor) from 2028 — an illustrative reading, not the legislative text, which is still going through co-decision.

From benchmark to allowances

A benchmark is a rate. This tab is about the resulting volume — how many allowances were actually handed out, and how that compares with what the covered installations actually emitted.

Free allocation = benchmark × historical activity level × carbon leakage exposure factor × CBAM factor × cross-sectoral correction factor

Only the first term is on this page. The others scale it: activity level is how much the installation actually produced, the leakage factor is 100% for listed sectors and falling to zero for everything else, the CBAM factor withdraws allocation for CBAM goods through 2034, and the correction factor cuts everyone back proportionally if the total would breach the cap.

Free allocation against verified emissions, 2005–2025

The benchmark values on this page set the rate. This is the resulting volume — how many allowances were actually handed out, against what the covered installations actually emitted. For the whole of phase 2 industry received more allowances than it emitted. The cliff in 2013 is the moment auctioning became the default: free allocation fell 59% in a single year, from 1 993 Mt to 825 Mt, while emissions barely moved.

Free allocation is now about 42% of verified emissions and still falling. That average hides the thing that matters: power generation receives almost nothing, while carbon-leakage industry receives close to its full benchmark entitlement — which is what the benchmark values on this page determine, one product at a time. The allowance price is the other half of the question: free allocation was worth little at €5 a tonne and is worth a great deal at €75, even as the volume has fallen by three quarters.

The reduction-rate methodology, and where it is exact vs. reconstructed

The derivation on the Reduction rates tab implements Article 10a(2) directly, for both 2021–2025 and 2026–2030. Three special cases are handled explicitly:

  • Aromatics and syngas are adjusted by the same percentage as refinery products (Art. 10a(2), fourth subparagraph), not by their own measured performance. The alignment uses the ratio of the published, rounded refinery benchmark to its 2007/08 base — that is what reproduces the official figures exactly.
  • Hydrogen was aligned with refineries for 2021–2025 but decoupled for 2026–2030, because Directive (EU) 2023/959 separated the two updates. It now uses its own measured rate.
  • Soda ash has relevant intermediate emissions added as a final step after the reduction is applied, per Reg. (EU) 2026/389. The formula alone gives 0,707; the published value is 1,122, so roughly 0,415 t CO₂e/t is the intermediate-emissions add-on.

The rate is a linear percentage of the 2007/08 value per year, not a compound rate — the Directive reduces "by 1% of the value that was set based on 2007-8 data in respect of each year".

A note on precision. The annual rates and raw reductions shown are derived from the annexes' published 10%-most-efficient averages, which are themselves rounded to three decimals. The Commission computed from the unrounded NIMs dataset, so treat the intermediate percentages as accurate to roughly a tenth of a point rather than exact. The benchmark values in the final column are the published figures, not derived ones.

Why some period-to-period comparisons are not like-for-like
  • Indirect emissions coverage (14 benchmarks). The benchmarks previously governed by fuel/electricity exchangeability now account for indirect emissions from electricity consumption. The Commission kept this coverage deliberately, to avoid penalising electrification; it raises the value, worth around €4 bn across 2026–2030. A rise here is not a weakened target.
  • Definition and system-boundary changes under Delegated Reg. (EU) 2024/873. "Sintered ore" became "Agglomerated iron ore"; grey and white cement clinker now cover alternative hydraulic binders; hot metal now covers direct-reduction steelmaking (though DRI output is excluded from the efficiency calculation, per Art. 10a(2)(e)); hydrogen and ammonia now cover hydrogen from water electrolysis.
  • Soda ash. See the note above. Reg. (EU) 2026/389 also amended the 2021–2025 soda ash value for the year 2025 specifically; the 0,753 shown here is the original 2021/447 figure.
  • Different data basis. 2013–2020 values came from voluntary 2007–2008 sector submissions, with five product benchmarks and both fall-backs derived from BREFs or literature. The later periods use verified installation-level data, so the P3→P4a step is partly a data-quality artefact — most visible across pulp and paper, where measured intensities collapsed to near zero.
  • Excluded sub-installations. Where emissions could not be attributed cleanly, installations were dropped from the calculation. For 2026–2030 this affects refinery products, hot metal, sintered dolime, ammonia, steam cracking, phenol/acetone and hydrogen.
Calculator assumptions
  • Activity level. The calculator treats your input as the historical activity level used for allocation. Real allocation uses a median of past activity and is adjusted when activity changes by more than 15% against the baseline — not modelled here.
  • CLEF for non-carbon-leakage sectors. Modelled as 30% in 2026 falling linearly to 0% in 2030 (30 / 22.5 / 15 / 7.5 / 0). The Directive says 30% "until 2026" then decreasing in equal amounts to 0% "after 2030", which some read as reaching zero in 2031 instead. If that reading prevails the figures for non-list sectors are slightly more generous than shown.
  • Beyond 2030 the benchmark is held flat at its 2026–2030 value because the 2031–2035 update has not been determined. Those rows are indicative.
  • EUA price is held flat. A single flat price is a modelling convenience, not a forecast, and the cost figures scale linearly with it.
  • Not modelled: the ETS investment booster, indirect cost compensation under state aid rules, CSCF changes over time, or the conditionality on decarbonisation investment plans proposed for 2031 onwards.
Units

Most product benchmarks are allowances / tonne of product. Refinery products and aromatics are allowances / CWT (CO₂-weighted tonne). Hydrogen and syngas are per tonne of 100% pure product. The heat and fuel fall-backs are allowances / TJ. Absolute values are only comparable within a unit class.

Known next steps in the policy
  • The Commission's ETS review of 17 July 2026 proposes sector-specific fall-back benchmarks, to raise allocation determined via the heat/fuel routes without triggering the cross-sectoral correction factor. If adopted, the single heat and fuel rows here would split by sector.
  • The same proposal would reintroduce 15% of phased-out free allocation for CBAM sectors from 2028 and extend the phase-out to 2038, extend carbon-leakage protection to 2040, define benchmark update rules for 2031–2035 and 2036–2040, raise the CSCF buffer to 4%, and from 2031 make all free allocation conditional on a verified decarbonisation investment plan.
  • No phase-out date has been set for carbon-leakage sectors outside CBAM.
