The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism

← Home EU ETS EU ETS Overview Free allocation benchmarks ETS 2 — buildings & transport Maritime Aviation CBAM Effort sharing Indirect costs Legislation map Energy map

A carbon price at the EU border, designed to rise in exact step with the withdrawal of free allowances from European industry. In its definitive regime since 1 January 2026.

The core idea, in one sentence

European producers have been shielded from the full carbon price by free allowances. CBAM removes that shield and replaces it with a border charge, so that domestic and imported goods face the same carbon cost — and so that the shield can be withdrawn without production simply moving abroad.

Certificates to surrender = embedded emissions × (1 − free allocation adjustment) − carbon price already paid in the country of production

The middle term is the interlock. As long as an EU producer of the same good still receives free allowances, the importer's obligation is reduced by the same proportion. The two move together by design — which is why the CBAM phase-in and the free allocation phase-out are the same schedule read from opposite ends.

The interlock: free allocation out, CBAM in

Both lines are governed by the CBAM factor in Article 10a(1a) of the ETS Directive. Free allocation to CBAM sectors falls to zero in 2034; the importer's obligation rises to 100% over exactly the same period.

The ETS review proposal tabled on 17 July 2026, COM(2026) 616, would reintroduce 15% of the phased-out free allocation from 2028 and stretch the phase-out to 2038. It is not law — it is in the ordinary legislative procedure, with agreement targeted for the first quarter of 2027. The dashed line is an illustrative reading of the published summary, not the legislative text.

Note that this change travels in the ETS Directive, not in the CBAM Regulation: the CBAM factor sits in Article 10a(1a) of Directive 2003/87/EC, so the instrument that slows CBAM down is an amendment to the ETS. The separate proposal of the same day, COM(2026) 619, is the fall-back benchmark uplift and has nothing to do with the CBAM factor.

Article 9: the carbon price already paid abroad

If a producer has already paid a carbon price in its own country, the importer may deduct it from the CBAM obligation. That is the provision which decides whether CBAM is a tariff or a genuine equalisation. Since 19 November 2026 the rules for doing so are in force: Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/2498, adopted on 5 November and applying back to 1 January 2026.

Three conditions

The price must be legally binding, actually paid rather than rebated, and evidenced. Crucially the deduction is assessed at installation level, not country level: two producers in the same country can end up with different deductions, and a national scheme existing on paper is not enough.

Now in force: Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/2498

In force since 19 November 2026 and applying from 1 January 2026, it governs the first declarations for 2026 imports, due by 30 September 2027. It does not recognise countries. Its Annex II lists, non-exhaustively, mechanisms that count as a carbon price — among them the ETSs of China, Japan (GX-ETS), Kazakhstan, Indonesia, South Korea, Türkiye, the UK, New Zealand, California, Washington and Oregon; baseline-and-credit systems in India, Australia and Canada; and carbon taxes in Chile, Singapore, South Africa, Ukraine, Taiwan and elsewhere — together with the rebates each one involves, such as free allowances. What can be deducted is still decided installation by installation, on certified evidence of what was actually paid. Being listed only settles that the mechanism qualifies. With free allocation of up to 100% in most of these systems, the net price actually paid will often be small: Türkiye's pilot, for instance, surrenders at 100% free allocation in 2027.

Two routes, tied to how emissions were determined. An importer using the producer's actual emissions can deduct the carbon price effectively paid, net of rebates and free allowances, certified by an accredited independent person — or use a default carbon price. An importer using default emission values can only use the Commission's default carbon price for the country; certified payment evidence cannot be paired with default emissions. The default prices and their methodology are to be published in the CBAM Registry before the first declarations. Article 6 Paris Agreement credits count for at most 10% of emissions. The method is on the How it is calculated tab.

The third leakage instrument

Free allocation and CBAM are locked together — as one falls the other rises. There is a third instrument that is not: compensation for indirect carbon costs, paid by member states to electricity-intensive industry for the carbon embedded in power prices. It has no phase-out date, no CBAM factor and no equivalent at the border, and it was widened in December 2025. It also explains CBAM's scope: indirect emissions are excluded from the border charge for exactly the goods whose EU producers receive that compensation. Full detail on compensation for indirect carbon costs.

What a certificate costs

Pegged to the ETS, not traded

CBAM certificates are not a market. The Commission sets the price administratively from the weighted average of EU ETS auction clearing prices, and sells them at that price. There is no secondary market, no banking beyond a limited carry-over, and no speculation — the price is simply the ETS price with a lag.

Quarterly now, weekly from 2027

For 2026 one price is set per calendar quarter, published in the week after the quarter ends. From 2027 it moves to a weekly average, which tracks the market more closely and reduces the timing arbitrage a quarterly figure creates. The methodology is set by Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2548.

Goods covered, and which emissions are counted

Six sectors, chosen for high carbon intensity and high trade exposure. The column that matters most is the third one. CBAM does not count indirect emissions — the carbon in the electricity used to make the goods — for every sector. Under Article 7 and Annex II of the Regulation, indirect emissions are excluded for the goods whose EU producers receive compensation for indirect carbon costs. The border charge mirrors the domestic treatment, sector by sector.

Sector CN codes Emissions countedWhy
This is the single largest gap in the mechanism. Primary aluminium's emissions are dominated by the electricity used in electrolysis, and steel's electric-arc route is almost entirely electrical — yet for both, CBAM counts only direct emissions. The transitional data shows aluminium at 5% of imported tonnage but 23% of embedded emissions; the part of that driven by electricity is currently outside the charge. The exclusion is explicitly framed as initial, and the Regulation obliges the Commission to gather what it needs to extend coverage to Annex II goods.

The symmetry is exact and worth stating plainly: the goods excluded from indirect coverage at the border are the goods whose EU producers are compensated for indirect costs at home. Change one and the case for the other changes with it — which is why the December 2025 widening of the state aid scheme matters for CBAM's scope, not just for industry's balance sheet.

Default values, and the penalty for using them

If an importer cannot obtain verified emissions data from its supplier, it may use official default values — published per country and per CN code in Annex I to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2621, as corrected by Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/1740 with effect from 1 January 2026. They are deliberately unattractive. A mark-up is added on top, rising each year, so the longer an importer relies on defaults the more it pays. The scheme is designed to make real supplier data the cheaper option.

Cement, aluminium, hydrogen, iron and steel:   +10% in 2026  →  +20% in 2027  →  +30% from 2028
Fertilisers:  +1% throughout

Defaults also vary sharply by origin, which is the whole point — the same product carries a different carbon charge depending on where it was made. Compare a good across countries below.

Defaults carry both direct and indirect emissions. The annex publishes a direct, an indirect and a total figure for every good, and the indirect component is the electricity used in production. That is the mirror image of the indirect cost compensation EU producers receive — and it is why the treatment of indirect emissions is the hinge on which the whole level-playing-field argument turns. For cement and fertilisers both parts are charged. For iron and steel, aluminium and hydrogen only the direct part is — the indirect segment is drawn hollow to show that it is published but not chargeable. See the Scope tab.

What the transitional period actually showed

CBAM ran as a reporting-only exercise from October 2023 to the end of 2025, and that produced the first hard evidence of what the mechanism covers. The Commission's review report of December 2025 puts roughly 156 million tonnes of CBAM goods imported between Q4 2023 and Q2 2025, and 167 million tonnes of CO₂e of embedded emissions accumulated in 2024 alone.

Aluminium is 5% of the tonnage and 23% of the emissions. Iron and steel dominate by weight — roughly 69% of everything imported — but aluminium carries more than four times its share of embedded carbon, because primary smelting runs on electricity. Cement is the mirror image: 11% of the mass, about 5% of the emissions. This is the clearest available argument for why the treatment of indirect emissions decides how much CBAM actually bites, and on which goods.

By volume the largest origins over the transitional period were Ukraine at about 14% and Türkiye at about 12% — a reminder that CBAM's first-order effects land on near neighbours and candidate countries rather than on distant economies.

The direction of travel

Downstream extension, now through both institutions

A December 2025 proposal would add roughly 180 steel- and aluminium-intensive downstream products from 1 January 2028. The logic is straightforward: charging carbon on imported steel but not on imported products made of steel simply moves the problem one step along the value chain.

Both co-legislators have now taken positions, and both went further than the Commission. The Council's general approach of 12 June 2026 keeps the Commission's list, adds about 200 further CN codes — just over 300 entries in all — and an annual review from 2028. Parliament adopted its negotiating position on 15 September 2026 by 464 votes to 50, pushing the list towards 457 products and adding technical assistance for developing countries and derogations for the outermost regions. Technical preparation began on 28 September 2026 and the first formal trilogue was held on 20 October 2026, at which both sides set out their mandates without reaching agreement. Four questions are reported to be open: how the 50-tonne threshold applies to light finished aluminium goods, whether pre-consumer scrap counts as a precursor, whether goods sold through online platforms are in scope, and what default values the added downstream goods get. The next round is set for 30 November, with political agreement targeted before the end of the year. The final list will sit somewhere above the original 180 rather than below it.

Anti-avoidance

The same proposal targets misdeclared emission intensities and the 'scrap loophole' — the possibility of routing material through pre-consumer steel and aluminium scrap, which currently carries no embedded emissions, to strip the carbon content out of a shipment on paper.

This is the part of the file least likely to be watered down. Scope is contested because it decides who pays; anti-circumvention is contested by almost nobody, since a mechanism that can be avoided on paper protects neither the climate nor the producers it was written for.

The downstream extension, drawn: proposal and positions

COM(2025) 989 is in trilogue, so there are three texts on the table: the Commission's proposal of 17 December 2025, the Council's general approach of 12 June 2026 and Parliament's mandate of 15 September 2026. From reporting exercise to full carbon border

CBAM has one of the longest run-ins of any EU instrument — eleven years from first report to full application, and possibly fifteen if the July 2026 proposal is adopted.

    Who is caught, and who is not

    The 50-tonne threshold

    Introduced by the October 2025 simplification, a single cumulative mass threshold: import 50 tonnes or less of CBAM goods in a year and the obligations do not apply. This was designed to remove the large majority of importers by number while retaining almost all of the covered emissions by volume.

    That design is not taken on trust. Article 2a(3) obliges the Commission to check that the threshold leaves out no more than 1% of embedded emissions, and to adjust it by delegated act if the real figure drifts. The first assessment, published 30 September 2026 and covering April 2025 to March 2026, put the exempted share at 0.87%. The threshold stands.

    Authorised declarant status

    Above the threshold, only an authorised CBAM declarant may import covered goods. That status must be applied for and granted in advance — it is not automatic, and importing without it is not a paperwork problem but a prohibition.

    Certificates are not allowances

    The word "certificate" invites the assumption that CBAM works like the ETS. It does not, and the differences are the things that catch importers out.

    The penalty does not discharge the obligation. An importer short by 500 tonnes pays €50 000 and still has to buy and surrender the 500 certificates. At the Q3 2026 certificate price of €82.32 that is roughly €182 per tonne all in — more than twice the carbon cost avoided. The €100 rate is deliberately identical to the ETS excess emissions penalty.

    Holding, buying back and cancellation

    The 50% quarterly minimum

    From 2027, at the end of every quarter an authorised declarant must hold certificates covering at least half of the embedded emissions accumulated since 1 January of that year — not since the start of the quarter. The base grows through the year. The Omnibus simplification cut this from 80% to 50% to ease the cash flow burden.

    Buyback and expiry

    Up to 50% of a year's purchases can be sold back to the national authority at the price originally paid, with a 31 October deadline. Anything beyond that must be surrendered or left to be cancelled — certificates expire on a roughly two-year cycle, so no importer can stockpile them as a hedge against a rising price.

    The compliance year

    The rhythm is retrospective: you import throughout a year, then buy and surrender certificates for those imports during the following year.

    WhenWhat happens
    Certificate sales for the 2026 compliance year begin in February 2027, and the declaration and surrender deadline is 30 September 2027. Goods imported during 2026 already carry an obligation, even though nothing has been bought or surrendered yet. The exposure accrues from the first import of the year; only the payment is deferred.

    Someone has to verify it, and there are not many of them yet

    A declaration for 2026 imports has to rest on verified emissions, and verification can only be done by a verifier accredited for the relevant activity group by a national accreditation body. That profession is being built from nothing while the first compliance year is already running.

    Five, as far as can be documented

    The first accreditation anywhere went to EmiCert in Greece in September 2026. Italy's Accredia followed with Bureau Veritas Italia, RINA Services and SGS ICS Italia, and Sweden's Swedac accredited Intertek Certification on 13 October 2026. The Commission's own list of verifiers had still not been published at that date, so a count has to be assembled from national accreditation registers and may be incomplete.

    Why the number matters

    Accredited verifiers can file their first verification reports in the CBAM Registry from January 2027, and every declaration for 2026 imports is due by 30 September 2027 — one nine-month window, for every authorised declarant in the Union at once. An installation's first verification also requires a site visit. Accreditation is granted per activity group rather than across the board, so a verifier accredited for steel is not thereby accredited for cement.

    Exposure by country of production

    CBAM's legal obligation sits with the EU importer, but its commercial weight lands on exporters — through renegotiated contracts and supplier switching. Two things determine how hard: the carbon intensity of a country's production, and how much carbon price its producers pay that can be deducted under Article 9.     What being listed in Annex II means

    It settles that the mechanism counts. Annex II to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/2498 names the emissions trading systems, baseline-and-credit schemes and carbon taxes that qualify as a “carbon price” under Article 9, and the rebates each one involves. An importer whose producer pays under a listed mechanism does not have to argue that it is a carbon price at all. The list is non-exhaustive: an unlisted mechanism can still qualify if it meets the criteria — binding on all covered operators, priced on emissions, and applied regardless of where the goods are sold. Russia, Brazil, Morocco, Egypt and Vietnam are not listed, and none of them currently charges producers of CBAM goods a carbon price.

    It is not country recognition, and it fixes no amount. Nothing is deducted at country level. The reduction is the price this producer effectively paid on these goods' embedded emissions, certified by an accredited independent person, with free allowances, exemptions, reduced rates and refunds subtracted first.

    The price column is the ceiling, not the deduction. It shows the headline price before any of those rebates. For industry, free allocation removes most of it: a plant receiving free allowances for 90% of its emissions under a €20 price pays about €2 per tonne. Türkiye's pilot allocates 100% free in 2027, so the net price there is zero; South Africa's tax allowances cut the R308 headline rate to roughly R15–46 per tonne. Even the UK, the highest-priced system listed, sits below the EU allowance price of around €82–83 in late November 2026, so the importer still owes the difference.

    With default emissions, a default price. An importer who uses the default emission values in the table above can only deduct the Commission's default carbon price for the country, not a certified figure. Those default prices are to be published in the CBAM Registry and were not yet available in November 2026.

    CBAM is being copied

    The mechanism is no longer unique to the EU, which changes the trade politics considerably.

    Norway — first mover

    Passed its own CBAM legislation in June 2026, adopting the EU mechanism into domestic law with a target start of 1 January 2027 — the first non-EU country to do so. As an EEA member already inside the ETS, alignment was the path of least resistance.

    United Kingdom and Australia

    The UK has its own CBAM scheduled for 1 January 2027, raising unresolved questions of mutual recognition between two separate carbon markets. At the EU–UK summit of 20 November 2026 the two sides announced that negotiations to link the UK ETS and the EU ETS had concluded. The aim is to apply the agreement from 2028, with allowances from either system valid in both from 1 January 2028, and to exempt each side's goods from the other's CBAM — around £6.5 bn of UK exports fall within the EU's. The legal text has not been published, and the agreement still has to be signed and concluded on both sides. A link would not by itself exempt anything: the Commission would have to list the UK by delegated act, open to objection by Parliament and Council, and the UK would need its own regulations to exclude EU goods. Until then both mechanisms apply in both directions. In Australia a government-commissioned Carbon Leakage Review recommended a staged border adjustment beginning with cement and clinker, with a decision due by mid-2027.

    What the UK will actually charge, and why it is a tenth of the EU figure

    On 16 October 2026 HMRC published the first illustrative rates for the UK mechanism, together with draft default values. They are not binding — the Revenue says they are not forecasts, the underlying free allocation data is still being checked, and the real rates for January to March 2027 are due before the end of this year. But they show the design clearly, and it is not the EU's.

    Set against an EU certificate price of €82.32 for the third quarter of 2026, those numbers look impossibly low, and the reason is structural. The EU sells certificates at the full allowance price and handles free allocation on its own side of the border, through the CBAM factor charted on the How it works tab. The UK instead nets free allocation out of the rate itself: the charge is the average UK ETS auction price for the preceding quarter — £58.15 for July to September 2026 — reduced by the share of emissions that the sector's own producers still receive free, scaled by the phase-out factor for the year. For cement that share is 87.1% and the 2027 factor is 97.5%, so an importer pays about 15% of the carbon price: 58.15 × [1 − (0.871 × 0.975)] ≈ 8.78. Fertilisers sit at £1.45 because their free allocation share is higher still.

    The two mechanisms are not comparable rate for rate. A low UK number does not mean a light UK mechanism, and the EU's high one does not mean the opposite: each is the carbon price minus whatever relief domestic producers in that jurisdiction still get, and the two jurisdictions are at different points on that path. The gap will close from the UK side as its free allocation is withdrawn. The comparison that matters for a given shipment is the rate against the goods' embedded emissions, which is why the UK also published default values — drawn from EU data recalculated to UK system boundaries, one trade-weighted value per good, with a 10% mark-up in every sector except fertilisers — for importers who cannot produce verified figures. That notice is still a draft.

    The obligation in one line

    For each declaration year the importer adds up its goods, and the number of CBAM certificates it must surrender is the emissions embedded in them, less two deductions. The first term depends on how the good was actually made — or on a default value if that is not known. The two deductions do not.

    Certificates = Σg ( SEEg × Mg ) − FAA − CP
    SEEg specific embedded emissions of good g, t CO₂e per tonne — measured or default
    Mg tonnes of good g imported in the year
    FAA free allocation adjustment — what an EU producer of the same good would still get free (Free allocation adjustment tab)
    CP carbon price effectively paid in the country of production, converted into certificates (Article 9)

    Each certificate then costs the CBAM certificate price for the period, which tracks the EU ETS auction price (How it works). The rest of this tab is about the first term: the two ways of arriving at SEEg.

    Two routes to SEE: measured or default

    Measured (actual) emissions

    The producing installation monitors its emissions under the methodology in Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2547, an accredited verifier checks them, and the figures reach the importer through the CBAM Registry. Annex IV of the CBAM Regulation gives the formula.

    Simple good (no precursors):
    SEEg = AttrEmg ÷ ALg
    Complex good:
    SEEg = ( AttrEmg + EEInpMat ) ÷ ALg,   EEInpMat = Σi Mi × SEEi

    AttrEmg are the emissions attributed to the good's own production process over the reporting period, ALg the tonnes produced, and Mi the tonnes of each precursor consumed, each carrying its own SEEi. Dividing through, the precursor term is mi × SEEi with mi = Mi ÷ ALg — the same Equation 5 that drives the free allocation adjustment. No mark-up applies.

    Default values

    Where verified data is not available, the importer takes the value for the good's CN code and country of origin from Annex I to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2621, as corrected by 2026/1740 (Countries tab), and adds a mark-up.

    SEEg = DVg,country × ( 1 + mark-upy )
    mark-up: 10% in 2026, 20% in 2027, 30% from 2028
                fertilisers: 1% throughout

    DV is the total emissions column: direct plus indirect for cement and fertilisers, direct only for iron and steel, aluminium and hydrogen, whose indirect emissions are outside CBAM. If the country is not listed or has no value, the ‘Other countries and territories’ row applies. Defaults are set at the average intensity of the origin country, or of the ten most carbon-intensive exporters where data is thin, and the mark-up is designed to make them more expensive than most real data.

    What goes into the measured figure

    ComponentHow it is counted
    Direct emissions Fuel combustion and process emissions within the system boundary of the production process — for clinker, the calcination of limestone and the kiln fuel. Emissions from heat the process imports are added; heat exported is deducted.
    Indirect emissions Electricity consumed × an emission factor. Counted only for cement and fertilisers. The two halves have different sources. The electricity consumed (MWh) is measured at the installation. The emission factor normally is not: it is the default grid factor of the country in Annex II to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2621, built from International Energy Agency data — 0.605 t CO₂/MWh for China, 0.420 for Türkiye, 0.726 for India (all sixteen origins on the Countries tab). A producer may use an actual factor instead only if it can show a direct technical link to a generator or a qualifying power purchase agreement. So even an importer with fully measured data usually carries a default inside its indirect emissions.
    Precursors The embedded emissions of relevant precursors, such as clinker in cement or pig iron in steel, carried into the good in proportion to the mass consumed. The producer may use actual data for its own process and default values for a bought-in precursor; where the precursor's country of production cannot be identified, the values in Annex IV of the default-values regulation apply.
    Reporting period Normally the calendar year, so SEE is an annual average for the installation, not a figure for the individual shipment.

    0.975 · 1.0 · €82.32 CBAM factor 2026, correction factor, certificate price Official — ETS Directive Art. 10a(1a); Decision (EU) 2026/1862; Commission's Q3 2026 price
    The same cement, a fourfold difference. Two things drive it. The default is an average for the whole country plus a mark-up, so it sits about twice as high as a good plant's real figure. And because the free allocation adjustment is capped by a benchmark rather than following the emissions, the whole of that gap lands on the certificates: the adjustment differs by only 130 t between the two routes, the embedded emissions by 834 t. The measured route also reflects the clinker ratio — a blended cement with less clinker has lower embedded emissions and a correspondingly smaller adjustment, which the single default for the CN code cannot distinguish.

    Is the default route's deduction more generous?

    On the deduction line, yes. The default-route benchmark for Portland cement (Column B, 0.666) is the clinker benchmark applied to a whole tonne of cement, as if it were 100% clinker; the measured route scales the same 0.666 by the clinker actually used. For the 80% cement above that is 0.13 t more deduction per tonne on defaults, worth about €10.70 in 2026 and half that by 2030, and nothing from 2034 when the adjustment ends. But the default route is consistent with itself: China's default for Portland cement carries the same 1.35 t of direct emissions as its default for clinker, so it too assumes a tonne of clinker per tonne of cement. The larger deduction comes with a larger charge. Defaults would only work out cheaper for a cement whose measured emissions exceeded about 1.43 t CO₂e/t, roughly twice what Portland cement emits.

    And grinding?

    It earns nothing, on either route. Grinding clinker into cement has no ETS benchmark — the Column A value for cement's own process is 0.000 — and electricity earns no free allocation in the ETS at all, because EU producers pay its carbon cost through the power price. So the indirect emissions, both the 0.024 t from grinding and the 0.039 t from electricity used to make the clinker, are charged in full with no deduction against them. The default route works the same way: its 0.666 benchmark covers direct emissions, while the 0.07 t of indirect emissions inside the 1.42 default goes undeducted.

    The second deduction: carbon price paid abroad

    Under Article 9 the importer may claim a reduction for a carbon price effectively paid in the country of production on the same embedded emissions. Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/2498, in force since 19 November 2026 and applying from 1 January 2026, turns that into a calculation:

    CP = ( Ppaid ÷ Pref,y ) × Mg
    Ppaid carbon price effectively paid per tonne of embedded emissions, in euros at the Commission's yearly average exchange rate, after deducting rebates — free allowances, emissions under a baseline, exempted emissions, reduced rates, refunds
    Pref,y the average CBAM certificate price for the year of import
    Mg tonnes of the good imported

    With measured emissions, Ppaid is the producer's own figure, certified by an accredited independent person (CBAM verifiers can do this for 2026 and 2027), or the Commission's default carbon price. With default emissions, only the default carbon price is available. International credits under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement count for at most 10% of the emissions, separately for direct and indirect; credits above that are valued at zero. Revenue-funded subsidies for cutting emissions are not treated as rebates if they meet six conditions, among them being open to every covered installation. In the cement example above CP is set to zero. China's national ETS is listed in Annex II and was extended to cement, steel and aluminium in 2025, but it allocates allowances free against intensity benchmarks, so the net price a cement plant actually pays — and with it CP — is likely to be small. The Commission will review the rules before the end of 2027; the status is on the How it works tab.

    Why free allocation is in the formula at all

    Before any of the equations make sense, one thing has to be settled: why an importer's bill depends on what the EU gives away to its own producers. The answer is that CBAM is not a tariff. It is a device for putting imported goods on the same carbon footing as EU goods — and EU goods are not currently paying the full carbon price either.

    An EU cement plant pays the ETS price on its emissions minus its free allocation.
    So an importer must pay the CBAM price on its emissions minus the same free allocation.
    Otherwise the import would be charged for tonnes the domestic producer gets for nothing.

    That subtraction is the free allocation adjustment. It is not a discount or a concession — it is the correction that keeps the two sides comparable. And because free allocation for CBAM goods is being withdrawn on a fixed schedule between 2026 and 2034, the adjustment shrinks every year. That is the entire mechanism by which CBAM ramps up: the border charge does not rise, the deduction falls.

    Two consequences follow, and both surprise people. First, the adjustment is calculated from EU data — a benchmark reflecting EU production, not the exporter's. Second, when EU industry's free allocation is cut for reasons that have nothing to do with trade, importers' bills rise automatically alongside. The equations below are just that idea made arithmetic.

    The free allocation adjustment, as the Regulation actually defines it

    Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2620 of 16 December 2025 sets out the calculation. It applies from 1 January 2026. What follows is its structure, taken from the text rather than reconstructed.

    FAAg = SEFAg,y × Mg   (Equation 1)
    the free allocation adjustment for a good is its specific embedded free allocation, times the mass imported

    Route A — default values: one equation and you are done

    SEFAg,y = CBAMy × CSCFy × BMg   (Equation 6)
    BM is an all-in figure per CN code: it already contains whatever allocation is embedded in the good's precursors.

    Route B — actual data: process first, then precursors

    This is where it gets misread. Declaring actual data does not give you a single benchmark. It builds the adjustment up the supply chain, one production step at a time.

    SFAProcg,y = CBAMy × CSCFy × BM*g   (Equation 2 — the good's own production step only)
    SEFAg,y = SFAProcg,y   (Equation 3 — simple good, no precursors)
    SEFAg,y = SFAProcg,y + Σi ( mi,y × SEFAi,y′ )   (Equation 4 — complex good, plus each precursor)
    mi,y = Mi,y ÷ ALy   (Equation 5 — how much of each precursor goes into one tonne of the good)
    Where a precursor is itself complex, Equations 2 to 5 repeat recursively until nothing is left.

    Equation 5, in words

    Equation 4 needs to know how much of each precursor sits inside a tonne of the good. Equation 5 supplies that number from the installation's own production records, not from a standard recipe:

    • Mi,y — the total mass of precursor i, in tonnes, consumed during the reporting period y by the production process that makes the good.
    • ALy — the activity level: the tonnes of goods produced within the system boundaries of that same production process over the same period.
    • mi,y — the ratio of the two: tonnes of precursor per tonne of good, the “specific mass”. It is not a mass fraction of the finished product: losses count, so if a mill consumes, say, 1.05 t of slab for every tonne of hot-rolled coil it ships, m is 1.05, not 1.0.

    Both quantities are measured at the level of the production process — the aggregated goods category — rather than shipment by shipment, and they come from the same monitoring that produces the embedded-emissions report (points E and F of Annex II to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2547), with the reporting period identified under Article 7 of that Regulation. The consequence is that the adjustment follows the actual input mix: a cement plant using less clinker per tonne gets a correspondingly smaller deduction — and is charged on correspondingly lower embedded emissions. The ratio always comes from the producer's records; what the annex allows to vary is the value attached to each precursor, SEFAi, which can come from the precursor's own reporting period or, where needed, from default values.

    What each term is

    Five terms across both routes. Three of them are fixed by EU policy and move for reasons that have nothing to do with the shipment; the last two come from the importer and its supplier.

    TermWhat it is Set byStatus

    BM* is therefore not comparable with BM. BM* covers one production step; BM covers the whole chain. A BM* of zero does not mean an adjustment of zero — it means that step earns nothing and everything comes through the precursor sum in Equation 4.

    Worked example: Portland cement with actual data

    Portland cement is the case that makes people think the adjustment vanishes, because BM* for cement is 0.000. Grinding clinker into cement is a milling operation: it earns no free allocation in the EU either, because there is no ETS benchmark for cement — only for clinker. Follow Equation 4 and the allocation reappears, in full, through the precursor.

    Step 1 — the cement's own process:
    SFAProccement = CBAM × CSCF × 0.000 = 0

    Step 2 — the clinker precursor, at roughly 0.8 t clinker per t cement:
    SEFAclinker = CBAM × CSCF × 0.666

    Step 3 — Equation 4 adds them:
    SEFAcement = 0 + ( 0.8 × CBAM × CSCF × 0.666 ) = CBAM × CSCF × 0.533

    With the 2026 CBAM factor of 0.975 and a correction factor of 1.0, that is an adjustment of about 0.52 t CO₂e deducted per tonne of cement imported. Not zero, and not far from what the default route would give you: the all-in default BM for Portland cement is 0.666, which assumes a clinker content of 100%.

    Which is why the clinker ratio is worth getting right. Under actual data your adjustment scales with the clinker you actually used. A blended cement at 65% clinker gets a smaller deduction than a pure CEM I at 95% — correctly, because it also has lower embedded emissions to be charged on in the first place. The default route ignores the distinction and applies one figure to everything under the CN code. The 0.8 ratio above is illustrative; the real value is the plant's own mclinker from Equation 5 — the tonnes of clinker it consumed in the year divided by the tonnes of cement it produced.

    So where do the measured emissions go?

    Nowhere in the equations above — and that is not an oversight. The free allocation adjustment is a deduction, benchmarked on what an EU producer would have received for nothing. It answers "how much would Europe have given away here?", which has nothing to do with how the imported good was actually made. Your measured emissions govern the other half of the calculation.

    certificates to surrender =
      embedded emissions × mass imported  ← your actual verified figure, or the default
      − free allocation adjustment  ← everything above; independent of your emissions
      − carbon price already paid in the country of origin  ← Article 9

    This is the whole point of the design. Because the deduction is fixed by the benchmark rather than by your performance, every tonne you abate is a tonne off the first line with nothing clawed back from the second. A producer half as carbon-intensive as the benchmark pays for half the emissions and receives the same adjustment — so being clean is worth exactly what it should be. If the adjustment tracked your own emissions instead, cleaning up your process would shrink your deduction too, and the incentive would partly cancel itself out. The first line — measured against default, with the same cement worked through both routes — is on the How it is calculated tab.

    The correction factor and its buffer

    The CSCF is a real multiplier, not a footnote. The cross-sectoral correction factor is determined under Article 14(6) of Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/331 and published under Article 10a(5) of the ETS Directive. If EU industry's benchmark entitlements exceed the free allocation ceiling, every EU installation is cut back — and because it sits directly in this equation, importers are cut back with them. A CSCF below 1 shrinks the adjustment and raises the CBAM bill.

    Only a fixed share of the cap may be given away free; the rest must be auctioned. Each period the Commission adds up what every installation is entitled to under its benchmarks. If that total comes in under the ceiling, everyone receives their full entitlement and the correction factor is 100% — which is what happened for 2021–2025. If it comes in over, the factor drops below 100% and every installation is scaled back by the same proportion, regardless of how efficient it is.

    What the buffer actually does

    Cutting everyone uniformly is a crude instrument, so the Directive provides a release valve before it has to be used. Under Article 10a(5a), an additional quantity — currently up to 3% of the total allowances issued — may be moved into the free allocation pot to close the gap. Those allowances come out of the auction share, so the buffer is paid for in forgone auction revenue rather than in cuts to industry.

    Entitlements under the ceiling → everyone paid in full, factor = 100%
    Entitlements over by less than the buffer → buffer drawn down, factor still 100%
    Entitlements over by more than the buffer → buffer exhausted, factor falls below 100%

    So the percentage is a headroom figure, not a cut. A 3% buffer means the ceiling can be overshot by up to 3% of total allowances before anyone loses anything. Widen it to 4%, as the July 2026 proposal would, and the overshoot has to be a quarter larger again before the correction factor bites. For 2021–2025 the buffer was not needed: demand for free allowances stayed below the ceiling, so none of the 3% was drawn and the factor stayed at 100%. For 2026–2030 it is doing real work. Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/1862 again sets the factor at 100% for every year, but only by carrying forward the 125.6 million allowances left unallocated in 2021–2025 and drawing about 289 million of the 368 million the 3% buffer provides over 2021–2030. The roughly 80–88 million left over is the headroom COM(2026) 619 spends on the fall-back benchmarks. The year-by-year ceiling, the derivation and the buffer figures are on the benchmarks page, Free allocation tab.

    Why an importer should care. A wider buffer makes a sub-100% correction factor less likely. Since the factor multiplies straight into the equations above, that keeps the free allocation adjustment larger and the CBAM bill correspondingly smaller. A change usually discussed as protection for EU industry lands on importers too — in the same direction, for once.

    CBAM benchmarks are not EU ETS benchmarks

    This is the part most easily got wrong. ETS benchmarks apply to sub-installations — hot metal, cement clinker, ammonia. CBAM benchmarks apply to goods, identified by CN code, and each one combines the relevant ETS product benchmarks with the heat, fuel and process-emissions fallbacks into a single figure for that commodity code. Sometimes the result is identical to the ETS value. Often it is not.

    CN codeGoodRoute / period BM* actual dataBM default valuesETS benchmark for comparison
    The two columns are not alternatives to compare. BM* covers a single production step and is used with actual data, where the precursors are then added separately through Equation 4. BM is all-in and is used with default values. Portland cement is the case that trips everyone: BM* is 0.000 because grinding clinker into cement earns no allocation of its own, while BM is 0.666 because the default figure carries the clinker with it. A zero in the BM* column does not mean no adjustment — it means the adjustment arrives entirely through the precursor. There is a worked example of exactly this case further up the tab.

    Three things worth knowing about these values

    Production route changes everything

    Primary aluminium carries a benchmark of 1.423; secondary, scrap-based aluminium carries 0.091 — a factor of fifteen. Steel is split six ways across BF/BOF, DRI/EAF and scrap/EAF routes for carbon, low-alloy and high-alloy grades. Where several alloy grades share a CN code, the Regulation directs use of the highest benchmark value.

    Some values change in 2028

    The factor used for process-emissions sub-installations under the ETS changes from 1 January 2028, so benchmarks resting on that fallback have one value for 2026–27 and another for 2028–30. Aluminous cement, for instance, moves from 0.717 to 0.686 — a smaller adjustment, and so a marginally larger obligation.

    These figures are provisional and the review is overdue. The final ETS benchmarks for 2026–2030 were not available when this Regulation was drafted, so the CBAM benchmarks were built on estimates of them, with a commitment to review them “at the latest one month” after the real values were published. Those arrived as Regulation (EU) 2026/1412, published on 29 June 2026 — the values on the benchmarks page — so the review was due by the end of July. As of mid-November 2026 no revised CBAM benchmark table has been adopted or published, and no draft has been put out for comment; the Commission's own CBAM Q&A of 15 October 2026 now says only that the benchmarks “should be reviewed shortly after” the final ETS values.

    What that means in practice. The revision is not retroactive: under recital 10 of Regulation (EU) 2025/2620 the updated benchmarks apply to goods imported from 1 January 2027. For goods imported in 2026 the values here remain the legally applicable ones. For 2027 imports they are placeholders until the revised table appears. Where a final ETS benchmark turned out lower than the estimate used here, the revised CBAM benchmark will be lower too, which shrinks the free allocation adjustment and raises the net cost per tonne — and with many ETS benchmarks cut by the maximum the rules allow, that is the direction to expect.

    Imported electricity gets no adjustment at all

    Article 1(2) is one line long and easy to miss: the free allocation adjustment for electrical energy under CN code 2716 00 00 shall be zero. Electricity generators in the EU receive no free allocation, so there is nothing to mirror — which means imported electricity has faced the full, undiscounted carbon price from 1 January 2026, while every other CBAM good is still 97.5% shielded. It is the only sector where CBAM is already operating at full strength.

    Where this simplifies
    • The free allocation adjustment is not exactly one minus the CBAM factor. The real mechanism is the Specific Embedded Free Allocation, set out in Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2620, which derives the reduction from the free allocation an equivalent EU installation would receive for that good — so it depends on the relevant benchmark, not only on the year. The calculator uses the CBAM factor as a proxy: right in shape, approximately right in level, not a substitute for the SEFA calculation.
    • Embedded emissions are more complicated than one number. They cover direct emissions and, for some goods, indirect emissions from electricity, plus emissions embedded in relevant precursors. Default values apply where actual data is not available, and they are deliberately conservative.
    • The Article 9 deduction is simplified. Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/2498, in force since 19 November 2026, sets the method: the net carbon price paid, divided by the yearly average certificate price and multiplied by the tonnes imported. The calculator approximates this by subtracting the € per tonne you enter from the certificate price. Enter the price net of rebates and free allowances, as the regulation requires; with default emission values, only the Commission's default carbon price may be used.
    • The certificate price is held flat. It follows the EU ETS, so in practice it moves. All cost figures scale linearly with it — halve the price and halve every number.
    The default values used here

    The calculator and the default value explorer both use the official CBAM default values from Annex I to the implementing regulation, version 1 as adopted on 4 February 2026 — not the corrected values issued by Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/1740 on 31 July 2026, which revise selected aluminium products and precursors of unknown origin with retroactive effect from 1 January 2026. For those goods in particular, check the corrected annex before relying on a figure here. Twenty origin countries and eleven representative goods are included — two cement, three fertiliser, hydrogen, three iron and steel and unwrought aluminium; the full annex covers around 120 countries and every CN code in scope, and should be consulted for anything you actually import.

    The calculator loads the total default (direct plus indirect) as a starting intensity, without the mark-up. If you are genuinely relying on defaults rather than supplier data, add the mark-up for the year in question — 10% in 2026 rising to 30% from 2028 for most goods, 1% throughout for fertilisers.
