The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism

← Home EU ETS Benchmarks CBAM Effort sharing Indirect costs Legislation map

A carbon price at the EU border, designed to rise in exact step with the withdrawal of free allowances from European industry. In its definitive regime since 1 January 2026.

The core idea, in one sentence

European producers have been shielded from the full carbon price by free allowances. CBAM removes that shield and replaces it with a border charge, so that domestic and imported goods face the same carbon cost — and so that the shield can be withdrawn without production simply moving abroad.

Certificates to surrender = embedded emissions × (1 − free allocation adjustment) − carbon price already paid in the country of production

The middle term is the interlock. As long as an EU producer of the same good still receives free allowances, the importer's obligation is reduced by the same proportion. The two move together by design — which is why the CBAM phase-in and the free allocation phase-out are the same schedule read from opposite ends.

The interlock: free allocation out, CBAM in

Both lines are governed by the CBAM factor in Article 10a(1a) of the ETS Directive. Free allocation to CBAM sectors falls to zero in 2034; the importer's obligation rises to 100% over exactly the same period.

The proposal tabled on 17 July 2026 would reintroduce 15% of the phased-out free allocation from 2028 and stretch the phase-out to 2038. It is not law — it is in the ordinary legislative procedure, with agreement targeted for the first quarter of 2027. Article 9: the carbon price already paid abroad

If a producer has already paid a carbon price in its own country, the importer may deduct it from the CBAM obligation. That is the provision which decides whether CBAM is a tariff or a genuine equalisation — and as of mid-2026 no country's scheme has been formally recognised.

Three conditions

The price must be legally binding, actually paid rather than rebated, and evidenced. Crucially the deduction is assessed at installation level, not country level: two producers in the same country can end up with different deductions, and a national scheme existing on paper is not enough.

Still not operational

The Commission published a draft implementing regulation setting out recognition pathways and the calculation method on 13 May 2026, with consultation closing 10 June 2026. It had not been adopted as of July 2026. South Korea's K-ETS, running since 2015, is generally regarded as the most advanced candidate — but it has no formal recognition either.

Until that regulation is adopted, the deduction field in any calculator — including the one on this page — is hypothetical. Importers can record a carbon price paid, but there is not yet an approved method for converting it into a reduced obligation.

The third leakage instrument

Free allocation and CBAM are locked together — as one falls the other rises. There is a third instrument that is not: compensation for indirect carbon costs, paid by member states to electricity-intensive industry for the carbon embedded in power prices. It has no phase-out date, no CBAM factor and no equivalent at the border, and it was widened in December 2025. It also explains CBAM's scope: indirect emissions are excluded from the border charge for exactly the goods whose EU producers receive that compensation. Full detail on compensation for indirect carbon costs.

What a certificate costs

Pegged to the ETS, not traded

CBAM certificates are not a market. The Commission sets the price administratively from the weighted average of EU ETS auction clearing prices, and sells them at that price. There is no secondary market, no banking beyond a limited carry-over, and no speculation — the price is simply the ETS price with a lag.

Quarterly now, weekly from 2027

For 2026 one price is set per calendar quarter, published in the week after the quarter ends. From 2027 it moves to a weekly average, which tracks the market more closely and reduces the timing arbitrage a quarterly figure creates. The methodology is set by Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2548.

Goods covered, and which emissions are counted

Six sectors, chosen for high carbon intensity and high trade exposure. The column that matters most is the third one. CBAM does not count indirect emissions — the carbon in the electricity used to make the goods — for every sector. Under Article 7 and Annex II of the Regulation, indirect emissions are excluded for the goods whose EU producers receive compensation for indirect carbon costs. The border charge mirrors the domestic treatment, sector by sector.

What a CN code is. The Combined Nomenclature is the EU's customs classification: an eight-digit code identifying exactly what a shipment contains, declared on every import. 7208 51 20, for instance, is hot-rolled steel plate of a particular thickness. CBAM's scope is defined entirely by these codes rather than by sector names — if a good's CN code is in Annex I of the Regulation it is covered, and if it is not, it is not. That makes the boundary precise but also arbitrary at the edges: two similar products can fall on opposite sides of it because of a customs distinction drawn long before CBAM existed.
SectorCN codes Emissions countedWhy
This is the single largest gap in the mechanism. Primary aluminium's emissions are dominated by the electricity used in electrolysis, and steel's electric-arc route is almost entirely electrical — yet for both, CBAM counts only direct emissions. The transitional data shows aluminium at 5% of imported tonnage but 23% of embedded emissions; the part of that driven by electricity is currently outside the charge. The exclusion is explicitly framed as initial, and the Regulation obliges the Commission to gather what it needs to extend coverage to Annex II goods.

The symmetry is exact and worth stating plainly: the goods excluded from indirect coverage at the border are the goods whose EU producers are compensated for indirect costs at home. Change one and the case for the other changes with it — which is why the December 2025 widening of the state aid scheme matters for CBAM's scope, not just for industry's balance sheet.

Default values, and the penalty for using them

If an importer cannot obtain verified emissions data from its supplier, it may use official default values — published per country and per CN code in Annex I to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2621, in the version adopted on 4 February 2026. They are deliberately unattractive. A mark-up is added on top, rising each year, so the longer an importer relies on defaults the more it pays. The scheme is designed to make real supplier data the cheaper option.

Cement, aluminium, hydrogen, iron and steel:   +10% in 2026  →  +20% in 2027  →  +30% from 2028
Fertilisers:  +1% throughout

Defaults also vary sharply by origin, which is the whole point — the same product carries a different carbon charge depending on where it was made. Compare a good across countries below.

Defaults carry both direct and indirect emissions. The annex publishes a direct, an indirect and a total figure for every good, and the indirect component is the electricity used in production. That is the mirror image of the indirect cost compensation EU producers receive — and it is why the treatment of indirect emissions is the hinge on which the whole level-playing-field argument turns. For cement and fertilisers both parts are charged. For iron and steel, aluminium and hydrogen only the direct part is \u2014 the indirect segment is drawn hollow to show that it is published but not chargeable. See the Scope tab.

What the transitional period actually showed

CBAM ran as a reporting-only exercise from October 2023 to the end of 2025, and that produced the first hard evidence of what the mechanism covers. The Commission's review report of December 2025 puts roughly 156 million tonnes of CBAM goods imported between Q4 2023 and Q2 2025, and 167 million tonnes of CO₂e of embedded emissions accumulated in 2024 alone.

Aluminium is 5% of the tonnage and 23% of the emissions. Iron and steel dominate by weight — roughly 69% of everything imported — but aluminium carries more than four times its share of embedded carbon, because primary smelting runs on electricity. Cement is the mirror image: 11% of the mass, about 5% of the emissions. This is the clearest available argument for why the treatment of indirect emissions decides how much CBAM actually bites, and on which goods.

By volume the largest origins over the transitional period were Ukraine at about 14% and Türkiye at about 12% — a reminder that CBAM's first-order effects land on near neighbours and candidate countries rather than on distant economies.

The direction of travel

Downstream extension, proposed

A December 2025 proposal would add roughly 180 steel- and aluminium-intensive downstream products from 1 January 2028. The logic is straightforward: charging carbon on imported steel but not on imported products made of steel simply moves the problem one step along the value chain.

Anti-avoidance

The same proposal targets misdeclared emission intensities and the 'scrap loophole' — the possibility of routing material through pre-consumer steel and aluminium scrap, which currently carries no embedded emissions, to strip the carbon content out of a shipment on paper.

From reporting exercise to full carbon border

CBAM has one of the longest run-ins of any EU instrument — eleven years from first report to full application, and possibly fifteen if the July 2026 proposal is adopted.

    Who is caught, and who is not

    The 50-tonne threshold

    Introduced by the October 2025 simplification, a single cumulative mass threshold: import 50 tonnes or less of CBAM goods in a year and the obligations do not apply. This was designed to remove the large majority of importers by number while retaining almost all of the covered emissions by volume.

    Authorised declarant status

    Above the threshold, only an authorised CBAM declarant may import covered goods. That status must be applied for and granted in advance — it is not automatic, and importing without it is not a paperwork problem but a prohibition.

    Certificates are not allowances

    The word "certificate" invites the assumption that CBAM works like the ETS. It does not, and the differences are the things that catch importers out.

    The penalty does not discharge the obligation. An importer short by 500 tonnes pays €50 000 and still has to buy and surrender the 500 certificates. At a €75 certificate price that is roughly €175 per tonne all in — more than twice the carbon cost avoided. The €100 rate is deliberately identical to the ETS excess emissions penalty.

    Holding, buying back and cancellation

    The 50% quarterly minimum

    From 2027, at the end of every quarter an authorised declarant must hold certificates covering at least half of the embedded emissions accumulated since 1 January of that year — not since the start of the quarter. The base grows through the year. The Omnibus simplification cut this from 80% to 50% to ease the cash flow burden.

    Buyback and expiry

    Up to 50% of a year's purchases can be sold back to the national authority at the price originally paid, with a 31 October deadline. Anything beyond that must be surrendered or left to be cancelled — certificates expire on a roughly two-year cycle, so no importer can stockpile them as a hedge against a rising price.

    The compliance year

    The rhythm is retrospective: you import throughout a year, then buy and surrender certificates for those imports during the following year.

    WhenWhat happens
    Certificate sales for the 2026 compliance year begin in February 2027, and the declaration and surrender deadline is 30 September 2027. Goods imported during 2026 already carry an obligation, even though nothing has been bought or surrendered yet. The exposure accrues from the first import of the year; only the payment is deferred.

    Exposure by country of production

    CBAM's legal obligation sits with the EU importer, but its commercial weight lands on exporters — through renegotiated contracts and supplier switching. Two things determine how hard: the carbon intensity of a country's production, and whether it has a carbon price that might one day be deductible under Article 9.     CBAM is being copied

    The mechanism is no longer unique to the EU, which changes the trade politics considerably.

    Norway — first mover

    Passed its own CBAM legislation in June 2026, adopting the EU mechanism into domestic law with a target start of 1 January 2027 — the first non-EU country to do so. As an EEA member already inside the ETS, alignment was the path of least resistance.

    United Kingdom and Australia

    The UK has its own CBAM scheduled for 2027, raising unresolved questions about mutual recognition between two linked-but-separate carbon markets. In Australia a government-commissioned Carbon Leakage Review recommended a staged border adjustment beginning with cement and clinker, with a decision due by mid-2027.

    Why free allocation is in the formula at all

    Before any of the equations make sense, one thing has to be settled: why an importer's bill depends on what the EU gives away to its own producers. The answer is that CBAM is not a tariff. It is a device for putting imported goods on the same carbon footing as EU goods — and EU goods are not currently paying the full carbon price either.

    An EU cement plant pays the ETS price on its emissions minus its free allocation.
    So an importer must pay the CBAM price on its emissions minus the same free allocation.
    Otherwise the import would be charged for tonnes the domestic producer gets for nothing.

    That subtraction is the free allocation adjustment. It is not a discount or a concession — it is the correction that keeps the two sides comparable. And because free allocation for CBAM goods is being withdrawn on a fixed schedule between 2026 and 2034, the adjustment shrinks every year. That is the entire mechanism by which CBAM ramps up: the border charge does not rise, the deduction falls.

    Two consequences follow, and both surprise people. First, the adjustment is calculated from EU data — a benchmark reflecting EU production, not the exporter's. Second, when EU industry's free allocation is cut for reasons that have nothing to do with trade, importers' bills rise automatically alongside. The equations below are just that idea made arithmetic.

    The free allocation adjustment, as the Regulation actually defines it

    Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2620 of 16 December 2025 sets out the calculation. It applies from 1 January 2026. What follows is its structure, taken from the text rather than reconstructed.

    FAAg = SEFAg,y × Mg   (Equation 1)
    the free allocation adjustment for a good is its specific embedded free allocation, times the mass imported
    SEFAg,y = CBAMy × CSCFy × BMg   (Equation 6, default values)
    SFAProcg,y = CBAMy × CSCFy × BM*g   (Equation 2, actual data, per production process)

    Three multiplied terms, and one of them is a benchmark that did not exist until December 2025. Note what is not in the equation: the importer's own emissions. The adjustment depends on the good, the year and the EU's allocation rules — not on how clean the exporting producer is. That is deliberate, and it is why a low-carbon producer keeps the full benefit of being low-carbon: the adjustment subtracts the same amount either way.

    The three terms

    TermWhat it is Set byStatus
    The CSCF is a real multiplier, not a footnote. The cross-sectoral correction factor is determined under Article 14(6) of Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/331 and published under Article 10a(5) of the ETS Directive. If EU industry's benchmark entitlements exceed the free allocation ceiling, every EU installation is cut back — and because it sits directly in this equation, importers are cut back with them. A CSCF below 1 shrinks the adjustment and raises the CBAM bill.

    The correction factor and its buffer

    Only a fixed share of the cap may be given away free; the rest must be auctioned. Each period the Commission adds up what every installation is entitled to under its benchmarks. If that total comes in under the ceiling, everyone receives their full entitlement and the correction factor is 100% — which is what happened for 2021–2025. If it comes in over, the factor drops below 100% and every installation is scaled back by the same proportion, regardless of how efficient it is.

    What the buffer actually does

    Cutting everyone uniformly is a crude instrument, so the Directive provides a release valve before it has to be used. Under Article 10a(5a), an additional quantity — currently up to 3% of the total allowances issued — may be moved into the free allocation pot to close the gap. Those allowances come out of the auction share, so the buffer is paid for in forgone auction revenue rather than in cuts to industry.

    Entitlements under the ceiling → everyone paid in full, factor = 100%
    Entitlements over by less than the buffer → buffer drawn down, factor still 100%
    Entitlements over by more than the buffer → buffer exhausted, factor falls below 100%

    So the percentage is a headroom figure, not a cut. A 3% buffer means the ceiling can be overshot by up to 3% of total allowances before anyone loses anything. Widen it to 4%, as the July 2026 proposal would, and the overshoot has to be a quarter larger again before the correction factor bites. In practice the buffer has never been needed: for 2021–2025 demand for free allowances stayed below the ceiling, so none of the 3% was drawn and the factor stayed at 100%.

    Why an importer should care. A wider buffer makes a sub-100% correction factor less likely. Since the factor multiplies straight into the equations above, that keeps the free allocation adjustment larger and the CBAM bill correspondingly smaller. A change usually discussed as protection for EU industry lands on importers too — in the same direction, for once.

    CBAM benchmarks are not EU ETS benchmarks

    This is the part most easily got wrong. ETS benchmarks apply to sub-installations — hot metal, cement clinker, ammonia. CBAM benchmarks apply to goods, identified by CN code, and each one combines the relevant ETS product benchmarks with the heat, fuel and process-emissions fallbacks into a single figure for that commodity code. Sometimes the result is identical to the ETS value. Often it is not.

    CN codeGoodRoute / period BM* actual dataBM default valuesETS benchmark for comparison
    Why there are two columns. BM* is used when actual emissions are declared, and covers only the process at that installation — the allocation embedded in precursors is added separately up the chain. BM is used with default values and is all-in. Portland cement is the clearest case: BM* is 0.000, because grinding clinker into cement earns no allocation of its own, while BM is 0.666, because the default figure carries the clinker with it. Reading the wrong column would understate or overstate the adjustment by the entire precursor.

    Three things worth knowing about these values

    Production route changes everything

    Primary aluminium carries a benchmark of 1.423; secondary, scrap-based aluminium carries 0.091 — a factor of fifteen. Steel is split six ways across BF/BOF, DRI/EAF and scrap/EAF routes for carbon, low-alloy and high-alloy grades. Where several alloy grades share a CN code, the Regulation directs use of the highest benchmark value.

    Some values change in 2028

    The factor used for process-emissions sub-installations under the ETS changes from 1 January 2028, so benchmarks resting on that fallback have one value for 2026–27 and another for 2028–30. Aluminous cement, for instance, moves from 0.717 to 0.686 — a smaller adjustment, and so a marginally larger obligation.

    These figures are provisional and a review is overdue. The final ETS benchmarks for 2026–2030 were not available when this Regulation was drafted, so the CBAM benchmarks were built on estimates of them, with a commitment to review within one month of the real values appearing. Those arrived in June 2026 as Regulation (EU) 2026/1412 — the values on the benchmarks page. Check for a revised CBAM benchmark table before relying on anything here.

    Imported electricity gets no adjustment at all

    Article 1(2) is one line long and easy to miss: the free allocation adjustment for electrical energy under CN code 2716 00 00 shall be zero. Electricity generators in the EU receive no free allocation, so there is nothing to mirror — which means imported electricity has faced the full, undiscounted carbon price from 1 January 2026, while every other CBAM good is still 97.5% shielded. It is the only sector where CBAM is already operating at full strength.

    Where this simplifies
    • The free allocation adjustment is not exactly one minus the CBAM factor. The real mechanism is the Specific Embedded Free Allocation, set out in Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2620, which derives the reduction from the free allocation an equivalent EU installation would receive for that good — so it depends on the relevant benchmark, not only on the year. The calculator uses the CBAM factor as a proxy: right in shape, approximately right in level, not a substitute for the SEFA calculation.
    • Embedded emissions are more complicated than one number. They cover direct emissions and, for some goods, indirect emissions from electricity, plus emissions embedded in relevant precursors. Default values apply where actual data is not available, and they are deliberately conservative.
    • The Article 9 carbon price deduction is not yet operational. The implementing regulation setting out recognition pathways was still in draft as of July 2026 and no third-country scheme has been formally recognised. The deduction field is therefore hypothetical — useful for modelling a future in which a scheme is recognised, not for a 2026 obligation.
    • The certificate price is held flat. It follows the EU ETS, so in practice it moves. All cost figures scale linearly with it — halve the price and halve every number.
    The default values used here

    The calculator and the default value explorer both use the official CBAM default values from Annex I to the implementing regulation, version 1 as adopted on 4 February 2026. Twenty origin countries and seven representative goods are included here; the full annex covers around 120 countries and every CN code in scope, and should be consulted for anything you actually import.

    The calculator loads the total default (direct plus indirect) as a starting intensity, without the mark-up. If you are genuinely relying on defaults rather than supplier data, add the mark-up for the year in question — 10% in 2026 rising to 30% from 2028 for most goods, 1% throughout for fertilisers.
