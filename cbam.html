The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism

← Home EU ETS Benchmarks CBAM Legislation map

A carbon price at the EU border, designed to rise in exact step with the withdrawal of free allowances from European industry. In its definitive regime since 1 January 2026.

The core idea, in one sentence

European producers have been shielded from the full carbon price by free allowances. CBAM removes that shield and replaces it with a border charge, so that domestic and imported goods face the same carbon cost — and so that the shield can be withdrawn without production simply moving abroad.

Certificates to surrender = embedded emissions × (1 − free allocation adjustment) − carbon price already paid in the country of production

The middle term is the interlock. As long as an EU producer of the same good still receives free allowances, the importer's obligation is reduced by the same proportion. The two move together by design — which is why the CBAM phase-in and the free allocation phase-out are the same schedule read from opposite ends.

The interlock: free allocation out, CBAM in

Both lines are governed by the CBAM factor in Article 10a(1a) of the ETS Directive. Free allocation to CBAM sectors falls to zero in 2034; the importer's obligation rises to 100% over exactly the same period.

The proposal tabled on 17 July 2026 would reintroduce 15% of the phased-out free allocation from 2028 and stretch the phase-out to 2038. It is not law — it is in the ordinary legislative procedure, with agreement targeted for the first quarter of 2027. The dashed line is an illustrative reading of the published summary, not the legislative text.

Article 9: the carbon price already paid abroad

If a producer has already paid a carbon price in its own country, the importer may deduct it from the CBAM obligation. That is the provision which decides whether CBAM is a tariff or a genuine equalisation — and as of mid-2026 no country's scheme has been formally recognised.

Three conditions

The price must be legally binding, actually paid rather than rebated, and evidenced. Crucially the deduction is assessed at installation level, not country level: two producers in the same country can end up with different deductions, and a national scheme existing on paper is not enough.

Still not operational

The Commission published a draft implementing regulation setting out recognition pathways and the calculation method on 13 May 2026, with consultation closing 10 June 2026. It had not been adopted as of July 2026. South Korea's K-ETS, running since 2015, is generally regarded as the most advanced candidate — but it has no formal recognition either.

Until that regulation is adopted, the deduction field in any calculator — including the one on this page — is hypothetical. Importers can record a carbon price paid, but there is not yet an approved method for converting it into a reduced obligation.

The third leakage instrument, and the one nobody phases out

Free allocation and CBAM are the two carbon leakage instruments this site has covered so far. There is a third, and it works differently from both: indirect cost compensation. Power generators pass their allowance costs into wholesale electricity prices, so an electricity-intensive manufacturer pays for carbon twice over — once on its own emissions, and again in its power bill. Member states may reimburse part of that second cost under EU State aid rules.

How big it is

In 2024, fifteen member states paid roughly €5.52 bn in compensation for indirect costs incurred in 2023 — up 40% on the €3.95 bn paid the year before. On the EEA's narrower measure of what came specifically out of 2024 auction revenue, the figure is €3.17 bn. Either way it is now one of the largest single uses of carbon revenue in the EU.

It was just widened, not narrowed

In December 2025 the Commission amended the State aid guidelines to add 20 sectors and two subsectors — including organic chemicals and parts of ceramics, glass and battery manufacturing — and raised the maximum share of indirect costs that may be compensated from 75% to 80%. Large recipients must now invest part of the aid in reducing electricity system costs, and the CO₂ factors used in the formula were updated for 2026–2030.

Free allocation → falling to zero by 2034, replaced one-for-one by CBAM
CBAM → rising to 100% over the same period, charged at the border
Indirect cost compensation → no phase-out date, and expanded in December 2025
This is why it matters for CBAM. The free allocation phase-out and the CBAM phase-in are locked together by design — as one falls the other rises, and the importer's obligation is reduced by exactly the protection the domestic producer still enjoys. Indirect cost compensation sits entirely outside that interlock. It has no phase-out schedule, no CBAM factor, and no equivalent adjustment at the border. So as free allocation disappears through 2034, compensation for indirect costs becomes the main remaining form of carbon leakage protection for electricity-intensive industry — and the one form CBAM does not mirror.

The disagreement runs in both directions. Industry associations argue that electricity costs are where European manufacturing is genuinely uncompetitive, and that the ETS should not penalise firms for electrifying. Analysts including Sandbag argue that widening the scheme diverts carbon revenue away from decarbonisation into untargeted support. Both are arguments about the same number, and it is now growing at 40% a year.

What a certificate costs

Pegged to the ETS, not traded

CBAM certificates are not a market. The Commission sets the price administratively from the weighted average of EU ETS auction clearing prices, and sells them at that price. There is no secondary market, no banking beyond a limited carry-over, and no speculation — the price is simply the ETS price with a lag.

Quarterly now, weekly from 2027

For 2026 one price is set per calendar quarter, published in the week after the quarter ends. From 2027 it moves to a weekly average, which tracks the market more closely and reduces the timing arbitrage a quarterly figure creates. The methodology is set by Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2548.

Goods currently covered

Six sectors, chosen for high carbon intensity and high trade exposure. Together they account for the bulk of the free allocation the ETS still hands out.

Default values, and the penalty for using them

If an importer cannot obtain verified emissions data from its supplier, it may use official default values — published per country and per CN code in Annex I to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2621, in the version adopted on 4 February 2026. They are deliberately unattractive. A mark-up is added on top, rising each year, so the longer an importer relies on defaults the more it pays. The scheme is designed to make real supplier data the cheaper option.

Cement, aluminium, hydrogen, iron and steel:   +10% in 2026  →  +20% in 2027  →  +30% from 2028
Fertilisers:  +1% throughout

Defaults also vary sharply by origin, which is the whole point — the same product carries a different carbon charge depending on where it was made. Compare a good across countries below.

Defaults carry both direct and indirect emissions. The annex publishes a direct, an indirect and a total figure for every good, and the indirect component is the electricity used in production. That is the mirror image of the indirect cost compensation EU producers receive — and it is why the treatment of indirect emissions is the hinge on which the whole level-playing-field argument turns. What the transitional period actually showed

CBAM ran as a reporting-only exercise from October 2023 to the end of 2025, and that produced the first hard evidence of what the mechanism covers. The Commission's review report of December 2025 puts roughly 156 million tonnes of CBAM goods imported between Q4 2023 and Q2 2025, and 167 million tonnes of CO₂e of embedded emissions accumulated in 2024 alone.

Aluminium is 5% of the tonnage and 23% of the emissions. Iron and steel dominate by weight — roughly 69% of everything imported — but aluminium carries more than four times its share of embedded carbon, because primary smelting runs on electricity. Cement is the mirror image: 11% of the mass, about 5% of the emissions. This is the clearest available argument for why the treatment of indirect emissions decides how much CBAM actually bites, and on which goods.

By volume the largest origins over the transitional period were Ukraine at about 14% and Türkiye at about 12% — a reminder that CBAM's first-order effects land on near neighbours and candidate countries rather than on distant economies.

The direction of travel

Downstream extension, proposed

A December 2025 proposal would add roughly 180 steel- and aluminium-intensive downstream products from 1 January 2028. The logic is straightforward: charging carbon on imported steel but not on imported products made of steel simply moves the problem one step along the value chain.

Anti-avoidance

The same proposal targets misdeclared emission intensities and the 'scrap loophole' — the possibility of routing material through pre-consumer steel and aluminium scrap, which currently carries no embedded emissions, to strip the carbon content out of a shipment on paper.

From reporting exercise to full carbon border

CBAM has one of the longest run-ins of any EU instrument — eleven years from first report to full application, and possibly fifteen if the July 2026 proposal is adopted.

    Who is caught, and who is not

    The 50-tonne threshold

    Introduced by the October 2025 simplification, a single cumulative mass threshold: import 50 tonnes or less of CBAM goods in a year and the obligations do not apply. This was designed to remove the large majority of importers by number while retaining almost all of the covered emissions by volume.

    Authorised declarant status

    Above the threshold, only an authorised CBAM declarant may import covered goods. That status must be applied for and granted in advance — it is not automatic, and importing without it is not a paperwork problem but a prohibition.

    Certificates are not allowances

    The word "certificate" invites the assumption that CBAM works like the ETS. It does not, and the differences are the things that catch importers out.

    The penalty does not discharge the obligation. An importer short by 500 tonnes pays €50 000 and still has to buy and surrender the 500 certificates. At a €75 certificate price that is roughly €175 per tonne all in — more than twice the carbon cost avoided. The €100 rate is deliberately identical to the ETS excess emissions penalty.

    Holding, buying back and cancellation

    The 50% quarterly minimum

    From 2027, at the end of every quarter an authorised declarant must hold certificates covering at least half of the embedded emissions accumulated since 1 January of that year — not since the start of the quarter. The base grows through the year. The Omnibus simplification cut this from 80% to 50% to ease the cash flow burden.

    Buyback and expiry

    Up to 50% of a year's purchases can be sold back to the national authority at the price originally paid, with a 31 October deadline. Anything beyond that must be surrendered or left to be cancelled — certificates expire on a roughly two-year cycle, so no importer can stockpile them as a hedge against a rising price.

    The compliance year

    The rhythm is retrospective: you import throughout a year, then buy and surrender certificates for those imports during the following year.

    WhenWhat happens
    Certificate sales for the 2026 compliance year begin in February 2027, and the declaration and surrender deadline is 30 September 2027. Goods imported during 2026 already carry an obligation, even though nothing has been bought or surrendered yet. The exposure accrues from the first import of the year; only the payment is deferred.

    Exposure by country of production

    CBAM's legal obligation sits with the EU importer, but its commercial weight lands on exporters — through renegotiated contracts and supplier switching. Two things determine how hard: the carbon intensity of a country's production, and whether it has a carbon price that might one day be deductible under Article 9.     CBAM is being copied

    The mechanism is no longer unique to the EU, which changes the trade politics considerably.

    Norway — first mover

    Passed its own CBAM legislation in June 2026, adopting the EU mechanism into domestic law with a target start of 1 January 2027 — the first non-EU country to do so. As an EEA member already inside the ETS, alignment was the path of least resistance.

    United Kingdom and Australia

    The UK has its own CBAM scheduled for 2027, raising unresolved questions about mutual recognition between two linked-but-separate carbon markets. In Australia a government-commissioned Carbon Leakage Review recommended a staged border adjustment beginning with cement and clinker, with a decision due by mid-2027.

    Where this simplifies
    • The free allocation adjustment is not exactly one minus the CBAM factor. The real mechanism is the Specific Embedded Free Allocation, set out in Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2620, which derives the reduction from the free allocation an equivalent EU installation would receive for that good — so it depends on the relevant benchmark, not only on the year. The calculator uses the CBAM factor as a proxy: right in shape, approximately right in level, not a substitute for the SEFA calculation.
    • Embedded emissions are more complicated than one number. They cover direct emissions and, for some goods, indirect emissions from electricity, plus emissions embedded in relevant precursors. Default values apply where actual data is not available, and they are deliberately conservative.
    • The Article 9 carbon price deduction is not yet operational. The implementing regulation setting out recognition pathways was still in draft as of July 2026 and no third-country scheme has been formally recognised. The deduction field is therefore hypothetical — useful for modelling a future in which a scheme is recognised, not for a 2026 obligation.
    • The certificate price is held flat. It follows the EU ETS, so in practice it moves. All cost figures scale linearly with it — halve the price and halve every number.
    The default values used here

    The calculator and the default value explorer both use the official CBAM default values from Annex I to the implementing regulation, version 1 as adopted on 4 February 2026. Twenty origin countries and seven representative goods are included here; the full annex covers around 120 countries and every CN code in scope, and should be consulted for anything you actually import.

    The calculator loads the total default (direct plus indirect) as a starting intensity, without the mark-up. If you are genuinely relying on defaults rather than supplier data, add the mark-up for the year in question — 10% in 2026 rising to 30% from 2028 for most goods, 1% throughout for fertilisers.
