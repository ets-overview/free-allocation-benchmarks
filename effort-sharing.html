The other two thirds of EU climate policy

The EU ETS gets most of the attention, but it covers roughly a third of EU emissions. Everything else — the car you drive, the boiler in your house, the cows in the field, the bin lorry — sits under the Effort Sharing Regulation. It is a fundamentally different instrument, and the difference matters.

EU ETS → one EU-wide cap, tradable allowances, the obligation on installations
Effort Sharing → 27 national targets, no market price, the obligation on governments

There is no carbon price here and no company holds an obligation. Member states are answerable for the total emissions of their transport, buildings, agriculture, small industry and waste sectors, and it is for each government to decide how to get there — through fuel taxes, building codes, subsidies, speed limits or anything else. The Regulation sets the destination and leaves the route entirely to national politics.

What it covers

In scope

Domestic transport excluding aviation, buildings, agriculture, small industry below the ETS thresholds, and waste. These are diffuse sectors with millions of emitters, which is precisely why a cap-and-trade system was never workable for them and why the obligation was placed on states instead.

The overlap to come

From 2028, ETS 2 will put a carbon price on fuels for buildings and road transport — sectors that remain under effort sharing. The same emissions will then sit under a national target and a carbon price simultaneously, with the price paid by fuel suppliers and the target owed by the government.

Two revisions, one direction

Effort sharing began as the Effort Sharing Decision (406/2009/EC) covering 2013–2020, replaced by Regulation (EU) 2018/842 for 2021–2030 and then revised by Regulation (EU) 2023/857 as part of Fit for 55. The 2023 revision raised the collective target from −30% to −40% and redistributed every national target upward — except one. All three acts, and the amendment chain between them, are on the legislation map.

How the targets were set

GDP per capita first

Targets are differentiated by GDP per capita, so richer member states carry deeper cuts. That is why Denmark, Germany, Luxembourg, Finland and Sweden all sit at the −50% ceiling while Bulgaria sits at the −10% floor. The principle is capacity to pay, not cost-effectiveness.

Then adjusted for cost

Pure GDP differentiation would leave some wealthy states paying far more per tonne than others, so targets above the EU average are adjusted back towards cost-effectiveness. The result is a compromise between fairness and efficiency that satisfies neither principle completely — which is roughly why the numbers look arbitrary until you know the recipe.

The 2023 increase was capped at 12 percentage points. Eight member states hit that ceiling: Belgium, Czechia, Germany, Ireland, Lithuania, the Netherlands, Austria and Slovenia. Without the cap their targets would have risen further, since the collective target rose by 11 points. A further +0.7 point adjustment was applied to states whose updated targets were not more ambitious than their cost-effective projections, to keep the EU-wide total intact.

Malta is the only member state whose target did not change, staying at −19%. It is also the only country granted a rising ETS flexibility allowance, from 2% to 7% from 2025 — a small state whose emissions are dominated by a handful of sources, handled as a special case at both ends of the Regulation.

Iceland and Norway

Both have agreed to implement the Regulation with binding 2030 targets, under the same rules, obligations and flexibilities as member states. Their targets have not been updated to match the 2023 increase in ambition, so they are still operating against the original 2018 numbers while the EU 27 work to the revised ones.

Five ways to meet a target without cutting emissions

A binding national target with no flexibility would be brittle: a cold winter or a recession would blow a hole in it. The Regulation therefore provides five escape valves, and understanding them is most of understanding how effort sharing actually works in practice.

The ETS flexibility, step by step

This is the only flexibility that reaches outside the effort sharing system altogether, and it is the one most often described loosely. It runs in three stages, and each stage is a different thing.

1. Cancel. The member state cancels EU ETS allowances that it would otherwise have auctioned.
2. Receive. It receives an equal quantity of effort sharing allocations, tonne for tonne.
3. Use. It may use those allocations towards its effort sharing target.

Who may use it, and up to how much

Nine member states qualify — those with targets well above their cost-effective potential, or which gave industry no free allocation in 2013 — plus Iceland and Norway, which implement the Regulation on the same terms. Each has a ceiling written into the Regulation, expressed as a percentage of its 2005 effort sharing emissions, applied in every single year from 2021 to 2030. That last point is the one most easily misread: a 2% state may take 2% of its 2005 emissions each year, so roughly 20% of one year's emissions across the full decade, not 2%.

4% ceiling — Ireland, Luxembourg, Iceland
2% ceiling — Belgium, Denmark, the Netherlands, Austria, Finland, Sweden, Norway
2% then 7% — Malta, whose ceiling itself rises from 2025
Within that ceiling, each state chooses its own percentage — and the published figures are what it chose, not what it has spent. The Commission describes the totals below as the maximum quantity a member state is allowed to use for effort sharing compliance. A state can notify 4%, forgo the auction revenue every year, and still never need to draw on the allocations. Whether any of it was actually required will only be visible at the five-yearly compliance checks.

The Netherlands took nothing

Eligible for 2% in every year, it notified zero throughout. Because its effort sharing sectors are the largest of the nine, its full entitlement would have been the biggest allocation in the group — larger than Ireland's 19.1 million, which is the largest anyone claimed. The Commission publishes the percentages, not the reasoning, so why is inference: the flexibility costs auction revenue every year, and a state expecting to meet its target domestically has no reason to pay.

Two changed their minds, in opposite directions

Denmark and Luxembourg used the 2024 revision window to cut their 2026–2030 percentages to zero. Sweden went the other way, but did so in its original 2019 notification, stepping up at 2025. Malta also steps at 2025 — but because its statutory ceiling rises then, not because it changed its mind. Hence the two different break points.

Two smaller things. Belgium's 1.89% is the only fraction in the table: every other participant either took its round ceiling or took nothing, which makes the ETS flexibility close to a binary choice in practice. And none of this is final — the nine have one last revision due by 31 December 2027, fixing the quantities for 2028–2030, and the Commission marks the totals above as subject to amendment on that basis.

What this does to the ETS is worth holding onto: the allowances are cancelled, not moved, so the ETS cap tightens by exactly the amount the effort sharing target loosens. It is one of the few mechanisms that shifts ambition between the two pillars without leaking any of it — which is also why choosing not to use it, as the Netherlands did, leaves the ETS looser than it would otherwise have been.

Do any of these flexibilities actually get used?

Everything above is a design, not a record. The current Regulation's first real compliance check has not happened, so nothing here has been tested. Its predecessor, the 2013–2020 Effort Sharing Decision, is finished — and it shared three of the mechanisms just described: banking, borrowing, and transfers between member states. It also had one the current Regulation does not, international credits. It did not have the ETS flexibility, which is genuinely new in 2021 and therefore has no track record at all.

For the other three, there is a complete record. Every bank, borrow, transfer and cancellation is a registry transaction, so they can be counted exactly. The Union Registry's effort sharing ledger holds 930 transactions covering the whole period, and all 224 member-state-year accounts balance to zero — nothing is missing. The answer it gives is not encouraging for anyone who expects flexibility to be where the action is.

What the registry shows about the rest

Banking did all the work

The collective surplus carried forward grew from 217 Mt after 2013 to a peak of 848 Mt after 2016, then hovered around 800 Mt to the end. Italy alone banked 202 Mt, Spain 147 Mt, the Netherlands 106 Mt, Greece 88 Mt. These were not states that overachieved through effort; the 2013–2020 targets were set against a 2005 baseline and then overtaken by the financial crisis. Banking is what let a badly calibrated budget stay technically intact.

Borrowing was used once

On 23 February 2022, Germany moved 14.87 million allocations from its 2020 account back into its 2019 account. That is the only carry-forward in the entire ledger. It is also a visible signal of distress: Germany's bank had fallen from 56 Mt in 2015 to zero by 2019, and having borrowed, it then had to buy.

International credits: attempted twice, used never

Member states could use Kyoto offsets for up to 3% of their 2005 emissions. Luxembourg moved 314,417 credits in during spring 2017 and returned them that May. Ireland moved 5,288,933 in during February 2022 and returned them the following September, buying allocations from Slovakia instead. Net use across the EU over eight years: zero.

Who ended up short

Only three member states ever needed to buy: Malta, Germany and Ireland. Every other state met its target from its own allocation and its own bank. Ireland and Malta were the two the Commission had flagged as off track for years; Germany was the surprise, and the only large economy in the group.

One thing this ledger cannot tell you. Allocations were cancelled both to cover emissions and voluntarily, and the registry records both as the same transaction type. Sweden cancelled its entire surplus every single year rather than banking it, France cancelled roughly 100 Mt in 2020, and Hungary cancelled its whole accumulated bank. A clean emissions series therefore cannot be derived from these figures, and none is presented here.

The safety reserve

A pool of up to 105 million tonnes available only in 2032, after the final compliance check, and only if the EU collectively hits its −40% target. It is a last resort for member states that miss despite everything else, and comes with strict conditions — including having overachieved between 2013 and 2020. A state that both misses its target and never banked anything from the previous decade cannot reach it.

Annual emission allocations

The 2030 target is the headline, but compliance is annual. Each member state receives a set number of annual emission allocations — one tonne of CO₂e each — for every year from 2021 to 2030, on a declining trajectory towards its 2030 target. Emissions above the allocation in any year create a shortfall to be covered by flexibilities.

2021–2022 allocations → set by Implementing Decision (EU) 2020/2126, unchanged by the revision
2023–2025 allocations → revised by Implementing Decision (EU) 2023/1319
2026–2030 allocations → determined in 2026, after a comprehensive review of emissions data

The 2026–2030 figures were still being set at the time of writing, on the basis of the 2025 comprehensive review of the 2021, 2022 and 2023 emissions estimates. An extra adjustment of 41 million tonnes was made for 2021 to ease the start for lower-income member states.

What happens if a member state misses

Excess emissions in a year  ×  1.08  →  added to the next year's emissions

There is no fine and no court case in the first instance. A shortfall is simply carried forward with an 8% surcharge, making the following year harder. A member state that is off track must submit a corrective action plan setting out additional measures, a timetable and the expected reductions.

The real check is every five years. The Commission reports annually on progress, but a comprehensive review of emissions reports and a formal compliance check happen on a five-year cycle, aligned with the Paris Agreement's global stocktake rhythm. That means a member state can drift for several years before the binding assessment lands — and the final check for the whole period comes only after 2030, with the safety reserve available in 2032.

Where it is all recorded

Allocations, transfers and flexibility use are tracked in an effort sharing registry being built inside the same Union Registry that holds ETS allowances. Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/109 of January 2026 amended the Registry Regulation specifically to make it work under the revised Effort Sharing Regulation — a reminder that the two systems share plumbing even though they work in completely different ways.

Everybody complied. That is the finding.

The Union Registry compliance data for the predecessor instrument — the Effort Sharing Decision, covering 2013–2020 — records every member state as compliant in every single year. 224 country-years, no penalties. Before reading that as a policy triumph, look at the aggregate.

Allocations exceeded emissions in every year of the period, by 95 to 292 Mt. Cumulatively the EU-28 was long by roughly 1.48 billion tonnes — 6.8% of the whole eight-year allocation, and equivalent to more than half a year of effort sharing emissions. Targets that everyone meets without difficulty were not targets that bound. That is the central criticism of the 2013–2020 framework, and the reason the 2021–2030 Regulation raised the collective ambition from −30% to −40%.

Who was actually stretched

Cumulative surplus or shortfall against allocations over 2013–2020, as a share of each country's total allocation. Hover for the number of individual years in which emissions exceeded that year's allocation.

Only three countries ended the period short: Ireland at −3.7%, Germany at −0.3% and Malta at −14.6% — and Malta exceeded its allocation in all eight years. At the other end, Greece was 25% under, Slovakia 19%, Hungary 19% and Croatia 18.5%. A surplus that large usually reflects economic conditions rather than climate policy: the 2013 baselines were set on pre-crisis projections that the following decade never delivered.

Everyone reached compliance regardless, because the flexibilities did their job — banking, borrowing and transfers absorbed every deficit that arose. The system worked exactly as designed. Whether it achieved anything the economy would not have delivered anyway is a different question, and this data cannot answer it.

What this does and does not tell you about 2021–2030

Not comparable

These are Effort Sharing Decision figures for a period with a −10% collective target, softer national targets and no ETS flexibility. The current Regulation demands −40%, redistributed upward across every member state. A clean record then implies very little about the decade now running.

But it sets the baseline

Banked surpluses from 2013–2020 are also the qualification for the safety reserve: a member state can only reach it in 2032 if it overachieved in the previous period. On this record most did, comfortably — which means the reserve is available to nearly everyone who might need it, rather than being the narrow backstop it sounds like.

The Regulation is deliberately silent on instruments, so the interesting variation — carbon taxes in some states, building regulation in others, agricultural measures almost nowhere — lives entirely in national policy and is not captured here.

A note on the 66% figure

The Commission describes effort sharing as covering around 66% of the EU's total domestic emissions, while the ETS is usually described as covering around 40% of EU greenhouse gas emissions. The two figures use different denominators — domestic emissions against total including international transport, and different treatment of LULUCF — so they do not sum to 100% and should not be subtracted from one another.
