Europe's carbon market in numbers: how many allowances exist, how fast that number is falling, how the surplus is managed, and what the system has actually delivered since 2005.

The cap, 2013 to 2040

Every allowance is a right to emit one tonne of CO₂e, and the cap is simply how many exist. It falls each year by a fixed number of allowances — the linear reduction factor applied to a fixed reference quantity — so the decline is a straight line, not a percentage curve. Two things make the recent years steeper: the reduction factor nearly doubled in 2024, and the cap was cut twice on top of that.

The single most consequential thing in this chart is not the slope but where the lines end. Extending today's reduction factor mechanically would take the cap to zero around 2039. The July 2026 proposal deliberately flattens the trajectory — 3.7% a year for 2031–35, then 1.7% — so allowances are still being issued in 2040. That is what aligns the ETS with a 90% net target rather than a 100% gross one.

The surplus and the Market Stability Reserve

The ETS spent its first decade drowning in unused allowances. The Market Stability Reserve, operating since 2019, withdraws allowances when the surplus is large and returns them when it is small. The measure it reacts to is the Total Number of Allowances in Circulation — every allowance ever issued, minus those surrendered or in the reserve.

TNAC, as published each May Upper threshold, 1 096 Mt — above this, 24% is withdrawn Upper buffer, 833 Mt — between the two, the excess over 833 is withdrawn Lower threshold, 400 Mt — below this, allowances are released

What the reserve actually did

Allowances go in, and since 2023 anything the reserve holds above 400 million is permanently cancelled rather than merely parked. That invalidation is what turns the MSR from a timing device into an instrument that genuinely removes supply — and it is exactly what the April 2026 proposal would stop.

What the 2026 proposals would change

Two separate proposals are in negotiation. Neither is law.

COM(2026) 153 — April 2026

Ends the invalidation mechanism, under which allowances held in the reserve above 400 million are permanently cancelled. They would instead be retained as a buffer against future tightness — a significant softening, since invalidation is what makes the MSR permanently remove supply rather than merely defer it.

COM(2026) 616 — July 2026

Recalibrates the reserve to a shrinking cap: a new lower buffer at 300 Mt from 2028, all reference points declining 4% a year from 2029, the intake rate halved from 24% to 12% with the upper threshold reset to 947 Mt, and cumulative net aviation demand folded into the TNAC — which alone cuts the 2027 figure by roughly 173 Mt and so triggers earlier releases.

Verified emissions by activity, 2005–2025

This is the most important chart on the page, and the least comfortable. Each shaded band is measured from that series' own lowest year up to its curve, so the depth of the shading is the reduction achieved. Hover any point for the gap in tonnes and in percent. Toggle series off to compare two at a time — the difference between combustion and industry is the whole argument about free allocation.

Combustion has fallen 54% since 2005. Industry has fallen 18%. A carbon price plus cheap renewables was enough to transform electricity generation. It has not been enough to transform steel, cement or chemicals — which is precisely why free allocation, the benchmark system and CBAM exist, and why the argument about them is so persistent. Aviation tells a third story: down 63% in 2020, then back to within a fifth of its 2012 level by 2025 — the only series here whose low point is behind it rather than at the end.

Auction revenue

Allowances not given away free are auctioned, and the money goes mostly to member states. From 2028 the July 2026 proposal would require at least 50% of it to go to defined climate priorities, with fossil fuel lock-in explicitly prohibited.

Revenue fell between 2023 and 2024 despite a tightening cap, because the allowance price fell further than the volume did. Auction revenue tracks price far more than it tracks ambition — a point worth remembering whenever the figure is cited as a measure of policy success.

The allowance price since 2005

The price is the whole mechanism: everything the ETS does, it does by making emissions expensive. Three eras are visible. A long slump through 2012–2017, when a post-crisis surplus left allowances trading between €4 and €8 and the carbon price changed almost nobody's investment decision. A steep climb from 2018, as the Market Stability Reserve began withdrawing that surplus and the 2030 target was tightened. Then a plateau from 2022 at levels an order of magnitude higher, where the price genuinely competes with the cost of abatement. Twenty years of scope changes

The ETS has grown by accretion. Each expansion brought a cap adjustment with it.

    Two systems, not one

    EU ETS 1 — since 2005

    Power generation, energy-intensive industry, intra-EEA aviation and, since 2024, maritime transport. Upstream of the consumer: the obligation sits with the installation or operator that emits. Roughly 40% of EU greenhouse gas emissions.

    EU ETS 2 — from 2028

    A separate, parallel system for fuels used in buildings, road transport and small industry. The obligation sits with the fuel supplier, not the household or driver, but the cost passes through at the pump and the boiler. Originally due in 2027 and postponed by a year in March 2026; auctioning still starts in 2027, and the Social Climate Fund is meant to cushion the distributional impact.

    Which numbers here are official and which are computed

    Official published figures: the annual caps for 2013–2024, the TNAC series, and the MSR intake volumes. The 2024 cap and the 2021 cap each come from a dedicated Commission decision.

    Computed by applying the legislated formula: the caps for 2025 to 2030. These are the fixed reference quantity of 2 039 152 882 allowances multiplied by the reduction factor for each period, with the 27 Mt rebasing subtracted in 2026. The method reproduces the 2026 cap of roughly 1 184 Mt cited in Commission material, and the 2030 result of about 827 Mt sits within a rounding error of the 62% reduction against 2005 that the Directive targets — two independent checks that the arithmetic is right.

    Illustrative: both post-2030 lines. The extension of current rules is a mechanical projection nobody intends to enact; the Phase 5 line applies the proposed reduction factors and ignores the cap increases the same proposal makes for carbon removals, aviation and maritime, so it understates the proposed cap somewhat. Treat both as showing shape, not level.

    Why the emissions figures need care — and why they differ from the EEA headline

    The emissions series is built from the EEA's EU ETS data viewer database (July 2026 release), using EU ETS verified emissions summed across the EEA — the EU 27 plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway and Northern Ireland. The United Kingdom is excluded throughout, so the pre-2021 years are comparable with the later ones. Activity codes follow the EEA's own grouping: 10 aviation, 20 combustion of fuels, 21–99 all industrial installations except combustion, 50 maritime transport. Combustion plus industry reconciles exactly to the EEA's own 20–99 stationary aggregate in every year, which is the check that the extraction is right.

    These are emissions as reported, not scope-corrected. The EEA's headline that stationary emissions fell 51% between 2005 and 2024 uses a scope-corrected series, which estimates what past emissions would have been under today's system boundaries. On a reported basis the same fall is 42%. The difference is large — the correction adds about 348 Mt to the 2005 baseline alone — and it exists because the ETS covered fewer activities and gases in its early years. Applying that correction reproduces the EEA's 51% figure to within a rounding error, which is why both numbers are right and neither is wrong.

    The correction is only published for the stationary aggregate, not per activity, so it cannot be applied to the four lines shown. Read the chart as the reported record. Two consequences follow: the pre-2013 aviation gap is real (aviation entered in 2012), and maritime starts in 2024 rather than being absent before it — its 91 Mt in the first year is new coverage, not new emissions.
