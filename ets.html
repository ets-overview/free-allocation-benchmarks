What the cap actually is

Worth settling before reading any chart on this page, because the word invites the wrong assumption. The cap is not a ceiling that emissions must stay under in a given year. It is the quantity of allowances issued in that year — a supply figure, fixed in advance to an exact number by Commission decision, and then created and distributed between auctioning and free allocation. 1 386 051 745 allowances in 2024, not "up to" that.

Cap = how many allowances are issued  ·  a budget, decided in advance
Not = how much may be emitted in that year  ·  that is only constrained cumulatively

Three things break the equivalence between the cap and a year's emissions, and all three matter when reading the charts below.

Allowances can be banked

Allowances do not expire within a trading period, so an installation can surrender one issued years earlier. Annual emissions can therefore exceed annual issuance. For most of the system's history the opposite happened — issuance ran ahead of emissions and accumulated as the surplus the Market Stability Reserve now exists to absorb. What binds is the cumulative total ever issued, minus whatever has been cancelled.

Issued is not the same as available

The MSR takes allowances out of auction volumes before they reach the market — around 190 Mt over the year from September 2026. Since 2023, anything the reserve holds above 400 Mt is cancelled outright, which is the only mechanism that permanently destroys supply rather than deferring it. So the cap overstates what is actually purchasable in any given year.

Aviation has its own separate cap, calculated bottom-up rather than from the reduction factor. The headline figure charted below is the cap for electricity and heat generation, industry and maritime transport. Comparing it directly against total verified emissions — which is a natural thing to do, and which the two charts on this page invite — mixes scopes. The comparison that is like for like is on the free allocation chart, where both series come from the same registry extract.

The cap, 2013 to 2040

Every allowance is a right to emit one tonne of CO₂e, and the cap is simply how many exist. It falls each year by a fixed number of allowances — the linear reduction factor applied to a fixed reference quantity — so the decline is a straight line, not a percentage curve. Two things make the recent years steeper: the reduction factor nearly doubled in 2024, and the cap was cut twice on top of that.

The single most consequential thing in this chart is not the slope but where the lines end. Extending today's reduction factor mechanically would take the cap to zero around 2039. The July 2026 proposal deliberately flattens the trajectory — 3.7% a year for 2031–35, then 1.7% — so allowances are still being issued in 2040. That is what aligns the ETS with a 90% net target rather than a 100% gross one.

Where the allowances go: auctioning and free allocation

The cap decides how many allowances exist. A separate set of rules decides who gets them, and on what terms. Every allowance issued leaves by one of two doors.

Cap  =  auctioned  (sold; the buyer pays the market price)
       +  freely allocated  (given away; benchmark × activity level × carbon leakage factor)
       +  set-asides  (Innovation and Modernisation Funds, market stability reserve, new entrants)

The split is not a policy dial someone turns each year. Free allocation is calculated bottom-up: each eligible installation's entitlement is its benchmark value times its historical activity level, adjusted for carbon leakage status. Auctioning is simply what is left. So when benchmarks tighten, free allocation falls and the auctioned share rises automatically — the two move as a seesaw inside a shrinking total.

Who gets nothing

Power generation receives essentially no free allocation and has bought its allowances since 2013. That is why the carbon price passes straight into electricity prices — and why electricity-intensive manufacturers are compensated separately for that pass-through through indirect cost compensation.

Who gets close to everything

Industry judged at risk of carbon leakage receives up to its full benchmark entitlement. That protection is now being withdrawn on a fixed schedule and replaced at the border by CBAM, reaching zero for CBAM goods in 2034.

Two limits sit on top. If all entitlements together would exceed the share of the cap available for free allocation, every installation is cut back proportionally by the cross-sectoral correction factor. And free allocation is not guaranteed for the life of an installation: it is adjusted when activity levels change materially, and surrendered if a plant closes.

The numbers behind this — free allocation against verified emissions from 2005, including the phase 2 years when industry received more allowances than it emitted, and the 59% cliff in 2013 when auctioning became the default — are on the benchmarks page, next to the benchmark values that determine them.

The surplus and the Market Stability Reserve

The ETS spent its first decade drowning in unused allowances. The Market Stability Reserve, operating since 2019, withdraws allowances when the surplus is large and returns them when it is small. The measure it reacts to is the Total Number of Allowances in Circulation — every allowance ever issued, minus those surrendered or in the reserve.

TNAC, as published each May Upper threshold, 1 096 Mt — above this, 24% is withdrawn Upper buffer, 833 Mt — between the two, the excess over 833 is withdrawn Lower threshold, 400 Mt — below this, allowances are released

What the reserve actually did

Allowances go in, and since 2023 anything the reserve holds above 400 million is permanently cancelled rather than merely parked. That invalidation is what turns the MSR from a timing device into an instrument that genuinely removes supply — and it is exactly what the April 2026 proposal would stop.

What the 2026 proposals would change

Two separate proposals are in negotiation. Neither is law.

COM(2026) 153 — April 2026

Ends the invalidation mechanism, under which allowances held in the reserve above 400 million are permanently cancelled. They would instead be retained as a buffer against future tightness — a significant softening, since invalidation is what makes the MSR permanently remove supply rather than merely defer it.

COM(2026) 616 — July 2026

Recalibrates the reserve to a shrinking cap: a new lower buffer at 300 Mt from 2028, all reference points declining 4% a year from 2029, the intake rate halved from 24% to 12% with the upper threshold reset to 947 Mt, and cumulative net aviation demand folded into the TNAC — which alone cuts the 2027 figure by roughly 173 Mt and so triggers earlier releases.

Verified emissions by activity, 2005–2025

This is the most important chart on the page, and the least comfortable. Each shaded band is measured from that series' own lowest year up to its curve, so the depth of the shading is the reduction achieved. Hover any point for the gap in tonnes and in percent. Toggle series off to compare two at a time — the difference between combustion and industry is the whole argument about free allocation.

Combustion has fallen 54% since 2005. Industry has fallen 18%. A carbon price plus cheap renewables was enough to transform electricity generation. It has not been enough to transform steel, cement or chemicals — which is precisely why free allocation, the benchmark system and CBAM exist, and why the argument about them is so persistent. Aviation tells a third story: down 63% in 2020, then back to within a fifth of its 2012 level by 2025 — the only series here whose low point is behind it rather than at the end.

Does anyone actually comply?

A cap is only as good as its enforcement, and this is the part of the system almost nobody looks at. For the 2024 compliance year the registry assigns each installation a code: A if it surrendered at least as many allowances as it emitted, B if it did not. Of 12 873 installations with a calculated status, 12 576 were compliant — 97.7%. A separate 475 fall under code C, meaning emissions were never reported at all, and 1 118 are excluded small emitters.

The gap sits almost entirely in the newest sectors. Aircraft operators come in at 91.2% and maritime at 96.4%, against 98–100% for the long-established stationary sectors — cement clinker, bulk chemicals, gypsum and mineral oil refining record no failures at all. Compliance appears to be a function of how long a sector has been in the system rather than how hard its abatement is, which is an argument for lead time whenever the scope expands again. Maritime entered in 2024 and is being measured in its very first compliance year.

Auction revenue

Allowances not given away free are auctioned. Revenue fell between 2023 and 2024 despite a tightening cap, because the allowance price fell further than the volume did — auction revenue tracks price far more than it tracks ambition, which is worth remembering whenever the figure is cited as a measure of policy success. Where the money goes is on the Revenue & spending tab.

Auction revenue, 2013–2024

Revenue is the allowance price multiplied by the volume auctioned, so it follows the price almost entirely. It grew tenfold between 2013 and 2023 — from €3.1 bn to €33.0 bn — then fell by a quarter in 2024 as the price came off, even though the cap kept tightening.

What the money is spent on

Member states must spend at least a defined share of auction revenue on climate and energy purposes, and report what they did with it. From the 2023 reporting year the EEA publishes a category breakdown; before that it was a single aggregate figure, so this view only exists for two years.

Two things stand out in 2024. €3.17 bn went back to industry as compensation for indirect carbon costs — the electricity price effect of the ETS — which is more than twice what was spent on social support and just transition combined. And €4.84 bn, a fifth of the year's revenue, was committed but not yet allocated, disbursed or reported. The carbon price raises money considerably faster than governments spend it.

Indirect cost compensation

€3.17 bn of 2024 auction revenue went back to industry as compensation for the carbon cost embedded in electricity prices — more than twice what was spent on social support and just transition combined. It is the third carbon leakage instrument alongside free allocation and CBAM, and the only one with no phase-out date. It now has its own page: compensation for indirect carbon costs.

Who spends it, and who does not

For 2024, revenue each member state was due to spend on climate and energy action, against what it reported actually spending. Hover for the share and for indirect cost compensation on top.

Across the EU 27, €16.3 bn of €21.7 bn was reported as spent — 75%. The gap is concentrated: Italy reported none of its €2.56 bn and Croatia none of its €118 m, while Estonia reported 8%, Latvia 6% and Lithuania 12%. Germany, the Netherlands, Bulgaria and Greece reported spending everything. France is its own case — it paid €834 m in indirect carbon cost compensation to industry, more than the €700 m it reported spending on climate and energy action. Reporting gaps are not the same as money vanishing: funds may be committed but not yet disbursed, or reported late. But the pattern is persistent enough to be a live criticism of how the revenue rule works.

The allowance price since 2005

The price is the whole mechanism: everything the ETS does, it does by making emissions expensive. Three eras are visible. A long slump through 2012–2017, when a post-crisis surplus left allowances trading between €4 and €8 and the carbon price changed almost nobody's investment decision. A steep climb from 2018, as the Market Stability Reserve began withdrawing that surplus and the 2030 target was tightened. Then a plateau from 2022 at levels an order of magnitude higher, where the price genuinely competes with the cost of abatement. Twenty years of scope changes

The ETS has grown by accretion. Each expansion brought a cap adjustment with it.

    Two systems, not one

    EU ETS 1 — since 2005

    Power generation, energy-intensive industry, intra-EEA aviation and, since 2024, maritime transport. Upstream of the consumer: the obligation sits with the installation or operator that emits. Roughly 40% of EU greenhouse gas emissions.

    EU ETS 2 — from 2028

    A separate, parallel system for fuels used in buildings, road transport and small industry. The obligation sits with the fuel supplier, not the household or driver, but the cost passes through at the pump and the boiler. Originally due in 2027 and postponed by a year in March 2026; auctioning still starts in 2027, and the Social Climate Fund is meant to cushion the distributional impact.

    How big is ETS 2 going to be?

    It does not start pricing until 2028, but reporting began in 2024 — and those reports are already in the Union Registry. Eighteen national accounts reported 957 Mt CO₂e for 2024. For scale, that is roughly 80% of everything the original ETS covered in the same year. ETS 2 is not a small addition to the carbon market; it is close to a second one.

    2024 only. The 2025 reporting is still coming in — 13 accounts and 307 Mt so far, which is incomplete rather than a collapse, so it is not shown. These are reported emissions under the monitoring obligation, not a cap: the ETS 2 cap is set separately and the first surrender obligation falls in 2029 for 2028 emissions.

    Which numbers here are official and which are computed

    Official published figures: the annual caps for 2013–2024, the TNAC series, and the MSR intake volumes. The 2024 cap and the 2021 cap each come from a dedicated Commission decision.

    Computed by applying the legislated formula: the caps for 2025 to 2030. These are the fixed reference quantity of 2 039 152 882 allowances multiplied by the reduction factor for each period, with the 27 Mt rebasing subtracted in 2026. The method reproduces the 2026 cap of roughly 1 184 Mt cited in Commission material, and the 2030 result of about 827 Mt sits within a rounding error of the 62% reduction against 2005 that the Directive targets — two independent checks that the arithmetic is right.

    Illustrative: both post-2030 lines. The extension of current rules is a mechanical projection nobody intends to enact; the Phase 5 line applies the proposed reduction factors and ignores the cap increases the same proposal makes for carbon removals, aviation and maritime, so it understates the proposed cap somewhat. Treat both as showing shape, not level.

    Why the emissions figures need care — and why they differ from the EEA headline

    The emissions series is built from the EEA's EU ETS data viewer database (July 2026 release), using EU ETS verified emissions summed across the EEA — the EU 27 plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway and Northern Ireland. The United Kingdom is excluded throughout, so the pre-2021 years are comparable with the later ones. Activity codes follow the EEA's own grouping: 10 aviation, 20 combustion of fuels, 21–99 all industrial installations except combustion, 50 maritime transport. Combustion plus industry reconciles exactly to the EEA's own 20–99 stationary aggregate in every year, which is the check that the extraction is right.

    These are emissions as reported, not scope-corrected. The EEA's headline that stationary emissions fell 51% between 2005 and 2024 uses a scope-corrected series, which estimates what past emissions would have been under today's system boundaries. On a reported basis the same fall is 42%. The difference is large — the correction adds about 348 Mt to the 2005 baseline alone — and it exists because the ETS covered fewer activities and gases in its early years. Applying that correction reproduces the EEA's 51% figure to within a rounding error, which is why both numbers are right and neither is wrong.

    The correction is only published for the stationary aggregate, not per activity, so it cannot be applied to the four lines shown. Read the chart as the reported record. Two consequences follow: the pre-2013 aviation gap is real (aviation entered in 2012), and maritime starts in 2024 rather than being absent before it — its 91 Mt in the first year is new coverage, not new emissions.
