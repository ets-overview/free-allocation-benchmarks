A second system, not an extension of the first

The EU ETS covers power stations, heavy industry, aviation and shipping — installations and operators large enough to be individually regulated. It never covered the boiler in a house or the fuel in a car, because there is no practical way to hand an allowance account to forty million households. ETS 2 solves that by moving the obligation upstream: it prices the same emissions, but charges the company that releases the fuel for consumption rather than the person who burns it.

EU ETS 1 = the emitter surrenders  ·  power, industry, aviation, maritime  ·  since 2005
EU ETS 2 = the fuel supplier surrenders  ·  buildings, road transport, small industry  ·  from 2028

The two systems have separate caps, separate allowances and separate registries. An ETS 2 allowance cannot be surrendered in ETS 1 and the prices are not linked. The Directive keeps them apart deliberately: the abatement costs in heating and transport are very different from those in steel, and merging them would let one sector's cheap reductions relieve pressure on the other.

Why upstream

The regulated entity is the one that releases fuels for consumption — broadly, the point at which excise duty falls due. That makes the population of regulated entities a few thousand fuel suppliers rather than tens of millions of end users, and it reuses an accounting infrastructure that already exists for tax purposes.

But paid downstream

The supplier buys the allowances and adds the cost to the price of heating oil, gas, petrol and diesel. Households and small businesses never touch the carbon market, but they pay for it at the pump and on the bill. That is the intended mechanism, and it is also the political problem the Social Climate Fund exists to answer.

Why it was postponed

ETS 2 was legislated to start pricing in 2027. In March 2026, as part of the package that adopted the 2040 climate target, the start was moved to 1 January 2028. The argument made for the delay was cost-of-living exposure: a carbon price landing on heating and motor fuels in a single step, in states where a large share of households heat with oil or gas, with the Social Climate Fund not yet fully disbursing.

Auctioning still begins in 2027. Only the surrender obligation moved. Allowances are sold from 2027 so that a market and a price exist before compliance starts, with the first surrender falling in 2029 for 2028 emissions. Monitoring and reporting have been running since 2024, and from 2026 the annual emissions reports must be verified by an accredited third party.

Where it sits against the rest of the framework

The emissions ETS 2 prices are the same emissions that already sit under national targets in the Effort Sharing Regulation. From 2028 a tonne of CO₂ from a German boiler is simultaneously a tonne a fuel supplier must hold an allowance for and a tonne counting against Germany's binding national target. That is deliberate — a price and a target pulling in the same direction — but it means the two instruments have to be read together, and that the revenue from ETS 2 accrues to the same government that owes the target.

What is covered

Annex III to the ETS Directive defines the scope by activity rather than by sector name: the release for consumption of fuels used for combustion in buildings, road transport and additional sectors — in practice, small industrial installations below the ETS 1 thresholds.

In scope

Heating fuels for residential and commercial buildings; petrol, diesel and other road transport fuels; and fuel combustion in industry not already covered by ETS 1, including installations under the 20 MW threshold.

Out of scope

Everything already in ETS 1, so no tonne is priced twice. Agriculture's non-energy emissions, waste, and the non-CO₂ gases stay outside — they remain purely effort-sharing territory. Rail and inland navigation are not part of road transport.

Who actually holds the obligation

The regulated entity is the natural or legal person releasing the fuel for consumption, or in some member states the person liable for the excise duty on it. Three consequences follow, and they are the things most often misunderstood about this system.

No free allocation is the quiet structural difference. ETS 1 hands out a large share of its allowances for nothing and auctions the rest. ETS 2 auctions all of them, which is why a system covering fewer tonnes than ETS 1 is expected to raise revenue on a comparable scale — and why the distributional question arrives immediately rather than in 2034.

How big is ETS 2 going to be?

Pricing does not start until 2028, but the monitoring obligation started in 2024 and those reports are already in the Union Registry. Eighteen national accounts reported 957 Mt CO₂e for 2024. For scale, that is roughly 80% of everything the original ETS covered in the same year. ETS 2 is not a small addition to the carbon market; it is close to a second one.

Three member states — Germany, France and Italy — account for about two thirds of what has been reported so far, which is roughly what their combined heating and road transport demand would suggest. Germany alone reports more than the next two together, and it is also the state with an existing national carbon price on the same fuels, which ETS 2 will replace.

2024 only, and incomplete. The 2025 reporting is still coming in — 13 accounts and 307 Mt at the last extract, which is incomplete rather than a collapse, so it is not shown. These are reported emissions under the monitoring obligation, not a cap: the ETS 2 cap is set separately, and the first surrender obligation falls in 2029 for 2028 emissions. Eighteen accounts is not twenty-seven member states, so the total understates full coverage.

The cap

ETS 2 has its own cap, set to bring the emissions it covers down 42% below 2005 by 2030. Like the ETS 1 cap it declines by a fixed quantity each year rather than by a percentage of the previous year, and it was built from reported effort-sharing data for the covered sectors rather than from a bottom-up count of installations.

Because the start moved to 2028, the quantities in the original 2023 text no longer line up with the years they were written for. The amending act rebases them. Anyone using these numbers for modelling should read the current consolidated text rather than the 2023 version — this is exactly the kind of detail a one-year postponement quietly breaks.

The price safeguard

ETS 2 has a mechanism ETS 1 does not: an explicit trigger that releases extra allowances if the price rises too far, too fast. It exists because the political tolerance for a carbon price on petrol and heating is far narrower than for one on industrial output.

If the average allowance price exceeds €45 (in 2020 prices) over a defined reference period, allowances are released from the ETS 2 market stability reserve.

Strengthened in June 2026

Council and Parliament reached a provisional agreement on 11 June 2026 that doubles the volume released when the €45 trigger fires, from 20 million allowances to 40 million, extends the reserve beyond 2030, and adds a more gradual release when the number of allowances in circulation falls below 260 million. The stated aim is to reduce volatility before the system ever charges anyone.

What it does not do

It is not a price cap. Releasing allowances adds supply and dampens a spike; it does not stop the price rising, and the released allowances come out of a finite reserve. A trigger set in 2020 prices also erodes in real terms if inflation runs ahead of the indexation, so the threshold is softer in 2028 than it looked in 2023.

The Social Climate Fund

ETS 2 and the Social Climate Fund were negotiated as a pair: the price is the instrument, the Fund is the condition on which it was politically possible. The Fund is financed from ETS 2 revenues and is expected to mobilise at least €86.7 bn between 2026 and 2032, combining the EU contribution with a mandatory 25% national co-financing share.

What it pays for

Structural measures first — building renovation, heating system replacement, access to zero-emission mobility — and temporary direct income support for vulnerable households and micro-enterprises as a bridge while those investments are made. Member states set out both in national Social Climate Plans.

The timing problem

Renovation and heating replacement take years; the fuel price moves the month the system starts. A fund that disburses slowly against a price that bites immediately is the central design risk, and the one-year postponement of ETS 2 buys the Fund exactly one more year of head start.

Whether the Fund is large enough is genuinely contested. Set against a system expected to raise revenue on the scale of ETS 1 auctioning, €86.7 bn over seven years is a meaningful share but not the whole of it, and the distribution between member states follows a formula based on exposure rather than on what each state actually raises.

From reporting exercise to carbon price

ETS 2 follows the pattern the ETS has used for every expansion: measure first, oblige later.

What this page does not cover

The detailed monitoring and reporting rules for fuel suppliers; the treatment of fuels used for purposes outside the covered sectors and the associated rebate mechanisms; the interaction with national carbon prices that ETS 2 replaces, particularly the German and Austrian schemes; and the per-member-state allocation of the Social Climate Fund.
