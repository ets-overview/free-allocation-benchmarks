A second system, not an extension of the first

The EU ETS covers power stations, heavy industry, aviation and shipping — installations and operators large enough to be individually regulated. It never covered the boiler in a house or the fuel in a car, because there is no practical way to hand an allowance account to forty million households. ETS 2 solves that by moving the obligation upstream: it prices the same emissions, but charges the company that releases the fuel for consumption rather than the person who burns it.

EU ETS 1 = the emitter surrenders  ·  power, industry, aviation, maritime  ·  since 2005
EU ETS 2 = the fuel supplier surrenders  ·  buildings, road transport, small industry  ·  from 2028

The two systems have separate caps, separate allowances and separate registries. An ETS 2 allowance cannot be surrendered in ETS 1 and the prices are not linked. The Directive keeps them apart deliberately: the abatement costs in heating and transport are very different from those in steel, and merging them would let one sector's cheap reductions relieve pressure on the other.

Why upstream

The regulated entity is the one that releases fuels for consumption — broadly, the point at which excise duty falls due. That makes the population of regulated entities a few thousand fuel suppliers rather than tens of millions of end users, and it reuses an accounting infrastructure that already exists for tax purposes.

But paid downstream

The supplier buys the allowances and adds the cost to the price of heating oil, gas, petrol and diesel. Households and small businesses never touch the carbon market, but they pay for it at the pump and on the bill. That is the intended mechanism, and it is also the political problem the Social Climate Fund exists to answer.

Why it was postponed

ETS 2 was legislated to start pricing in 2027. In March 2026, as part of the package that adopted the 2040 climate target, the start was moved to 1 January 2028. The argument made for the delay was cost-of-living exposure: a carbon price landing on heating and motor fuels in a single step, in states where a large share of households heat with oil or gas, with the Social Climate Fund not yet fully disbursing.

Auctioning still begins in 2027. Only the surrender obligation moved. Allowances are sold from 2027 so that a market and a price exist before compliance starts, with the first surrender falling in 2029 for 2028 emissions. Monitoring and reporting have been running since 2024, and from 2026 the annual emissions reports must be verified by an accredited third party.

Where it sits against the rest of the framework

The emissions ETS 2 prices are the same emissions that already sit under national targets in the Effort Sharing Regulation. From 2028 a tonne of CO₂ from a German boiler is simultaneously a tonne a fuel supplier must hold an allowance for and a tonne counting against Germany's binding national target. That is deliberate — a price and a target pulling in the same direction — but it means the two instruments have to be read together, and that the revenue from ETS 2 accrues to the same government that owes the target.

What is covered

Annex III to the ETS Directive defines the scope by activity rather than by sector name: the release for consumption of fuels used for combustion in buildings, road transport and additional sectors — in practice, small industrial installations below the ETS 1 thresholds.

In scope

Heating fuels for residential and commercial buildings; petrol, diesel and other road transport fuels; and fuel combustion in industry not already covered by ETS 1, including installations under the 20 MW threshold.

Out of scope

Everything already in ETS 1, so no tonne is priced twice. Agriculture's non-energy emissions, waste, and the non-CO₂ gases stay outside — they remain purely effort-sharing territory. Rail and inland navigation are not part of road transport.

Who actually holds the obligation

The regulated entity is the natural or legal person releasing the fuel for consumption, or in some member states the person liable for the excise duty on it. Three consequences follow, and they are the things most often misunderstood about this system.

No free allocation is the quiet structural difference. ETS 1 hands out a large share of its allowances for nothing and auctions the rest. ETS 2 auctions all of them, which is why a system covering fewer tonnes than ETS 1 is expected to raise revenue on a comparable scale — and why the distributional question arrives immediately rather than in 2034.

How big is ETS 2 going to be?

Pricing does not start until 2028, but the monitoring obligation started in 2024 and those reports are already in the Union Registry. Eighteen national accounts reported 957 Mt CO₂e for 2024. For scale, that is roughly 80% of everything the original ETS covered in the same year. ETS 2 is not a small addition to the carbon market; it is close to a second one.

Three member states — Germany, France and Italy — account for about two thirds of what has been reported so far, which is roughly what their combined heating and road transport demand would suggest. Germany alone reports more than the next two together, and it is also the state with an existing national carbon price on the same fuels, which ETS 2 will replace.

2024 only, and incomplete. The 2025 reporting is still coming in — 13 accounts and 307 Mt at the last extract, which is incomplete rather than a collapse, so it is not shown. These are reported emissions under the monitoring obligation, not a cap: the ETS 2 cap is set separately, and the first surrender obligation falls in 2029 for 2028 emissions. Eighteen accounts is not twenty-seven member states, so the total understates full coverage.

The other thing the registry shows: the accounts are being opened now

Reported emissions are not the only ETS 2 trace in the Union Registry. Fuel suppliers caught by the system need a regulated entity holding account to surrender from 2028. On 26 November 2026 there were 2 106 of them, in twenty-one member states and Norway, none closed or flagged for closure. The first was opened on 5 June 2026; the monthly run is 25 in June, 92 in July, 124 in August, 333 in September, 512 in October and 1 020 in November to the 25th. The two Union-level accounts that auctioning needs, a regulated entity auction account and a total quantity account, were both opened on 14 October 2026.

Germany did it in a day. All 712 German accounts were opened on 6 November 2026, which made Germany the largest registry overnight, with a third of the Union total. Ninety-three of them carry "nEHS" or "BEHG" in their names, the labels of Germany's national fuel emissions trading scheme, which ETS 2 will replace: the pattern points to an existing population of suppliers being set up in bulk rather than one by one. Italy follows with 527, built up steadily since June, then Austria 149, Czechia 115, Belgium 103 and Slovakia 101.

The gaps are now few but notable. Poland, Romania, Portugal, Hungary, Estonia and Cyprus have opened none. Spain (5) and Malta (3) opened their first in November, and France, the second-largest emitter in the 2024 reports, has 28. Account numbers depend on how each state defines the regulated entity and whether suppliers are grouped, so they are not a measure of coverage; but a state with no accounts thirteen months before the system starts has the most left to do.

The cap

ETS 2 has its own cap, designed to bring the emissions it covers down 42% below 2005 by 2030. Like the ETS 1 cap it falls by a fixed number of allowances each year rather than by a percentage of the previous year's cap. Article 30c of the ETS Directive sets it in two steps.

1 · A starting line from inventory data

Before 2024 nobody measured these emissions at the level of fuel suppliers, so the first line is drawn from the effort-sharing inventory: the 2024 value is the emission limit for the covered sectors, and it falls by 5.10% of that 2024 value a year, about 62.4 million allowances. The Commission fixed the 2027 point at 1 036 288 784 allowances (Decision (EU) 2024/2951); the line reaches about 849 million in 2030. Because the system starts in 2028, 2028 is the first year for which allowances are actually issued: no 2027 allowances exist, and the 2027 figure is only a point on the line.

2 · Rebased on what fuel suppliers report

The cap from 2028 starts again from the average emissions reported for 2024 to 2026, placed at 2025, and falls by 5.38% of that value a year — the same absolute reduction as the first line, expressed against its 2025 point. The Commission publishes the 2028 cap by 30 June 2027. If reported emissions come in more than 2% above the line's 2025 value, the factor is raised so that the cap still reaches the line's 2030 value (Annex IIIa); if they come in lower, 5.38% applies and the cap ends lower too.


The slider starts at the line's own 2025 value, 1 161 million, where the two steps coincide. The 2026 figure will not be reported until 2027, and the 957 Mt on the How big it is tab covers only the eighteen national accounts that had reported for 2024, so it is not a basis for the 2028 cap. One exception in the annex is not modelled: if the gap between reported and inventory emissions for 2025 is under 5% and explains the excess, the 5.38% factor stays.

Why two steps. The inventory line was the only basis available when the law was written, but inventory categories and fuel suppliers' reports do not match exactly. Rebasing on reported data makes the cap fit what is actually monitored and surrendered. The asymmetry is deliberate: a starting point that turns out higher than expected cannot loosen the 2030 endpoint, while one that turns out lower tightens it.

The price safeguard

ETS 2 has a mechanism ETS 1 does not: an explicit trigger that releases extra allowances if the price rises too far, too fast. It exists because the political tolerance for a carbon price on petrol and heating is far narrower than for one on industrial output.

If the average auction price exceeds €45 (in 2020 prices) for two consecutive months, 40 million allowances are released from the ETS 2 market stability reserve.

The reserve and its price triggers

ETS 2 has its own market stability reserve, separate from the one serving ETS 1, set up by the 2023 directive with 600 million allowances. Three price conditions release allowances from it. The €45 trigger (in 2020 prices, adjusted for inflation) releases 40 million when the average auction price stays above it for two consecutive months: 20 million under Article 30h(2) of the ETS Directive and a further 20 million added by the MSR Decision. It applies from the start of auctioning until the end of 2029. Independently of the price level, a rapid rise releases 50 million if the average price over more than three consecutive months is more than twice the average of the six months before (1.5 times in 2027 and 2028), and 150 million at more than three times. Releases are not added together, and after any release nothing more is injected for twelve months, with one exception: if the price is above €45 again in the second half of that year, the €45 trigger can fire a second time. That is why it can deliver up to 80 million a year. Every release is spread evenly over three months, starting no later than two months after the Commission publishes that the condition has been met.

Why it is built this way

The design answers one worry: a price spike on petrol and heating oil in the first years, before households and fuel suppliers have adjusted. Up to 80 million allowances a year at €45 is more than the 60 million by which the cap falls each year, so the reserve can more than offset the tightening while the trigger applies. The sliding band between 210 and 260 million replaces a single cliff, where a small change in circulation decided between no release and 100 million. Keeping all 600 million allowances valid beyond 2030 means the buffer exists for as long as the system does, rather than ending just as the €45 trigger lapses. The Commission's review of the reserve must weigh what remains in it against environmental integrity and social fairness. These rules are set by Decision (EU) 2026/2402, in force since 17 November 2026.

And the start date is still 2028

It was questioned at the highest level in October 2026 and survived. At the European Council of 15–16 October 2026, Poland, Czechia, Slovakia and Hungary, supported by Italy and Greece, pressed to postpone ETS 2 to 2030 or later; a majority, including the Nordic states, the Netherlands, France and Germany, refused. The conclusions do not move the date. They ask the Commission instead to "update the assessment of all impacts of ETS 2, in particular its design and safeguards" (paragraph 8), and separately call on the co-legislators to accelerate work on the ETS 1 market stability reserve (paragraph 7). An impact assessment is not a delay, but it is the instrument through which one would be argued for, so it is worth watching what the Commission comes back with.

What it does not do

It is not a price cap. Releasing allowances adds supply and dampens a spike; it does not stop the price rising, and the released allowances come out of a finite reserve. A trigger set in 2020 prices also erodes in real terms if inflation runs ahead of the indexation, so the threshold is softer in 2028 than it looked in 2023.

The price triggers, drawn: what a price path would set off

Pick a price path for the first two and a half years of auctions. The chart applies the three price triggers month by month and shows, as bars, what the reserve would release. The paths are illustrations, not forecasts, and prices are in 2020 euros, the unit the €45 threshold is written in.

How the chart decides. The €45 trigger fires in the second of two consecutive months above €45 and releases 40 million. The rapid-rise trigger compares the average of the latest three months with the average of the six months before them: above 1.5 times in 2027 and 2028, or twice from 2029, it releases 50 million; above three times, 150 million. If the €45 condition is met together with a rapid-rise condition, only the rapid-rise release counts (Article 30h(4)). After any release, no further release under the article can follow for twelve months (Article 30h(6)) — which also blocks the 150 million tier, as the extreme spike shows. The one exception is a second €45 release when the price is above €45 again in the second half of that period, which the Commission may allow by implementing act (Article 30h(7)); the chart assumes it does. The 1.5-times factor is written for 2027 and 2028. Simplifications: one price per month instead of the auction-by-auction average, and no inflation adjustment, which in nominal euros puts the threshold well above €45 by 2028.

The volume triggers, drawn: how the reserve responds

Alongside the price triggers, the reserve reacts to one number: the allowances in circulation, published each year by 1 June from 2028 — every ETS 2 allowance issued, minus verified emissions since 1 January 2028, minus allowances cancelled. For any value on the horizontal axis, the chart shows how many allowances the reserve takes in (above zero) or gives back (below zero).

Reading the shape. Above 440 million the reserve takes in 100 million over the following twelve months. Between 260 and 440 million nothing happens. Between 260 and 210 million the release rises in a straight line, 100 million minus twice the amount by which circulation exceeds 210 million — 50 million at 235 million — and below 210 million it is a flat 100 million. Releases are added to auction volumes from 1 September. The reserve starts with 600 million allowances and none of them expires after 2030, so the release side of the chart stays available for the life of the system.

The Social Climate Fund

ETS 2 and the Social Climate Fund were negotiated as a pair: the price is the instrument, the Fund is the condition on which it was politically possible. The Fund is financed from ETS 2 revenues and is expected to mobilise at least €86.7 bn between 2026 and 2032, combining the EU contribution with a mandatory 25% national co-financing share.

What it pays for

Structural measures first — building renovation, heating system replacement, access to zero-emission mobility — and temporary direct income support for vulnerable households and micro-enterprises as a bridge while those investments are made. Member states set out both in national Social Climate Plans.

The timing problem

Renovation and heating replacement take years; the fuel price moves the month the system starts. A fund that disburses slowly against a price that bites immediately is the central design risk, and the one-year postponement of ETS 2 buys the Fund exactly one more year of head start.

Whether the Fund is large enough is genuinely contested. Set against a system expected to raise revenue on the scale of ETS 1 auctioning, €86.7 bn over seven years is a meaningful share but not the whole of it, and the distribution between member states follows a formula based on exposure rather than on what each state actually raises.

From reporting exercise to carbon price

ETS 2 follows the pattern the ETS has used for every expansion: measure first, oblige later.
