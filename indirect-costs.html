Why it exists

Power generators hold no free allowances, so they buy every allowance they need and pass the cost into wholesale electricity prices. An electricity-intensive manufacturer therefore pays for carbon twice: once on its own emissions, and again in its power bill. The second cost is invisible in the ETS accounts and falls hardest on exactly the sectors — aluminium, some chemicals, electric-arc steel — whose competitors abroad face no equivalent.

Member states may reimburse part of that second cost, under Commission State aid guidelines that set who qualifies and how much may be paid. It is therefore not an EU instrument in the way the ETS is: Brussels sets the ceiling, capitals decide whether to pay at all.

The three leakage instruments, side by side

Free allocation → falling to zero by 2034, replaced one-for-one by CBAM
CBAM → rising to 100% over exactly the same schedule, charged at the border
Indirect cost compensation → no phase-out date, and expanded in December 2025
The first two are locked together by design — the same schedule read from opposite ends. Indirect cost compensation sits entirely outside that interlock. It has no phase-out schedule, no CBAM factor and no equivalent at the border. So as free allocation disappears through 2034, it becomes the main remaining form of carbon leakage protection for electricity-intensive industry, and the one form CBAM does not mirror.

What changed in December 2025

Wider and more generous

The Commission amended the State aid guidelines to add 20 sectors and two subsectors — including organic chemicals and parts of ceramics, glass and battery manufacturing — and raised the maximum compensable share of indirect costs from 75% to 80%. Member states may notify further sectors if they can show a genuine leakage risk.

With conditions attached

Large recipients must now contribute to the green transition, including by investing a share of the aid in projects that reduce electricity system costs. The CO₂ factors and geographic zones used in the compensation formula were updated for 2026–2030.

The driver is the carbon price, not policy generosity

Compensation is calculated from the allowance price of the previous year, so the curve is a lagged echo of the ETS price. The Commission attributes the 2020 doubling and the 2021 jump directly to that: the carbon price used for 2021 compensation was €25.20, against €16.15 for 2019. The single dip, in 2022, followed a year when the price barely moved.

Most schemes have no fixed budget. They reimburse incurred costs up to a ceiling, so the bill rises automatically with the very price signal the ETS exists to create. A successful carbon price and a growing compensation bill are the same phenomenon — which is why this instrument cannot be assumed to shrink on its own.

Who pays it

Payments made in 2024 for costs incurred in 2023, by member state. Hover for the share of that state's 2023 auction revenue the compensation represents.

Three countries account for 71% of it. Germany paid €2.40 bn, France €0.91 bn and Poland €0.61 bn. But the sharper measure is compensation as a share of auction revenue: Luxembourg paid 492% of its ETS auction revenue, France 44% and Germany 32%, while Italy paid under 5% and Portugal 3%. Because the schemes are national and voluntary, a member state with fiscal room can shield its industry and one without cannot — inside a single market the ETS is meant to keep level.

Where the money comes from, and why it is hard to see

Member states report annually, under the Governance Regulation, how they spend ETS auction revenue — 677 named projects across 27 countries for 2024. Searching that dataset for indirect cost compensation returns only five countries and about €1.04 bn, against €5.52 bn actually paid out.

The gap is structural, not an error. Compensation is deducted from auction revenue before the Article 10(3) spending obligation applies — €3.2 bn of 2024 revenue, 13% of the total, was set aside this way — so for most member states it never appears as a reported use of revenue at all. The rest is paid from general budgets. The result is that the EU's own revenue-reporting dataset shows less than a fifth of the money.

There is no category for it either. Of the five countries that do report it as a project, four book it under "15. Other" and Slovenia books it under "14. Administrative expenses". The reporting taxonomy has fourteen substantive categories — energy efficiency, public transport, industry decarbonisation, adaptation and so on — and none of them fits the single largest transfer back to industry.

The symmetry with CBAM scope

Under Article 7 and Annex II of the CBAM Regulation, indirect emissions are excluded from the border charge for precisely the goods whose EU producers receive compensation for indirect costs.

The two columns are mirror images, and deliberately so: the Regulation's recitals say indirect emissions should not initially be counted for goods in respect of which financial measures apply in the Union that compensate for indirect emission costs. Change one and the case for the other changes with it — which is why December 2025's widening is a CBAM scope question, not only an industrial subsidy question.

The argument

The case for

Electricity costs are where European manufacturing is genuinely uncompetitive, and the ETS should not penalise firms for electrifying. A carbon price that makes clean electricity expensive relative to gas abroad pushes exactly the wrong way. Industry associations, including the chemicals sector, pressed for wider eligibility on these grounds through 2025.

The case against

Analysts including Sandbag argue that widening the scheme diverts carbon revenue away from decarbonisation into untargeted support, and that compensation paid per tonne of electricity consumed weakens the incentive to consume less of it. In 2024 the payments exceeded what member states spent on social support and just transition combined.

Two figures, both correct, not comparable

€3.2 bn — the revenue basis

What member states reported financing out of 2024 ETS auction revenue, 13% of the total collected. This is the basis used in the revenue breakdown on the EU ETS page, where it sits alongside every other spending category.

€5.52 bn — the disbursement basis

Total compensation paid out during 2024 by fifteen member states, for costs incurred in 2023, from whatever source. Larger because it is not limited to ETS revenue and runs on a different year basis.

The two differ by roughly three quarters. Whenever you see a figure for indirect cost compensation, check which basis it uses. The trajectory chart on this site uses the disbursement basis throughout, so its years are comparable with each other.
