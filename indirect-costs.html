Why it exists

Power generators hold no free allowances, so they buy every allowance they need and pass the cost into wholesale electricity prices. An electricity-intensive manufacturer therefore pays for carbon twice: once on its own emissions, and again in its power bill. The second cost is invisible in the ETS accounts and falls hardest on exactly the sectors — aluminium, some chemicals, electric-arc steel — whose competitors abroad face no equivalent.

Member states may reimburse part of that second cost from national budgets, under Commission State aid guidelines that set who qualifies and how much may be paid. It is therefore not an EU instrument in the way the ETS is: Brussels sets the ceiling, capitals decide whether to pay at all. That asymmetry is itself contested, because a member state with fiscal room can shield its industry and one without cannot.

The three leakage instruments, side by side

Free allocation → falling to zero by 2034, replaced one-for-one by CBAM
CBAM → rising to 100% over exactly the same schedule, charged at the border
Indirect cost compensation → no phase-out date, and expanded in December 2025

The first two are locked together by design. The free allocation phase-out and the CBAM phase-in are the same schedule read from opposite ends, and the importer's obligation is reduced by exactly the protection the domestic producer still enjoys — the mechanics are on the CBAM page.

Indirect cost compensation sits entirely outside that interlock. It has no phase-out schedule, no CBAM factor, and no equivalent adjustment at the border. So as free allocation disappears through 2034, compensation for indirect costs becomes the main remaining form of carbon leakage protection for electricity-intensive industry — and the one form CBAM does not mirror.

The symmetry with CBAM scope

This is the part that is easy to miss and hard to unsee. Under Article 7 and Annex II of the CBAM Regulation, indirect emissions are excluded from the border charge for precisely the goods whose EU producers receive compensation for indirect costs.

The two columns are mirror images, and that is deliberate: the Regulation's recitals say indirect emissions should not initially be counted for goods in respect of which financial measures apply in the Union that compensate for indirect emission costs. Change one and the case for the other changes with it — which is why December 2025's widening of the state aid scheme is a CBAM scope question, not only an industrial subsidy question.

The trajectory, 2017 to 2024

Compiled from eight successive Commission reports on the functioning of the European carbon market. Each figure is the total aid paid out in that year, for indirect costs incurred the year before. It has grown roughly eightfold in seven years.

The driver is the carbon price, not policy generosity. Compensation is calculated from the allowance price of the previous year, so the curve is a lagged echo of the ETS price. The Commission attributes the 2020 doubling and the 2021 jump directly to that: the carbon price used for 2021 compensation was €25.20, against €16.15 for 2019. The single dip, in 2022, came after a year when the price barely moved. Since most schemes have no fixed budget and simply reimburse incurred costs up to a ceiling, the bill rises automatically with the very price signal the ETS exists to create.

Who pays it

Payments made in 2024 for costs incurred in 2023, by member state, against that state's 2023 auction revenue. The right-hand figure is the share of auction revenue the compensation represents.

Three countries account for well over half of it. Germany alone paid €2.40 bn, France €0.91 bn and Poland €0.61 bn. But the more revealing column is the share of auction revenue: Luxembourg paid 492% of its ETS auction revenue in compensation, France 44% and Germany 32%, while Italy paid under 5% and Portugal 3%. Because schemes are national and voluntary, a member state with fiscal room can shield its industry and one without cannot — inside a single market that the ETS is meant to keep level.

What changed in December 2025

Wider and more generous

The Commission amended the State aid guidelines to add 20 sectors and two subsectors — including organic chemicals and parts of ceramics, glass and battery manufacturing — and raised the maximum share of indirect costs that may be compensated from 75% to 80%. Member states may also notify further sectors for approval if they can show a genuine leakage risk.

With conditions attached

Large recipients must now contribute to the green transition, including by investing a share of the aid in projects that reduce electricity system costs. The CO₂ emission factors and geographic zones used in the compensation formula were also updated for 2026–2030.

Two figures, both correct, not comparable

Reporting on this topic mixes two different measures, which is worth knowing before quoting either.

€3.17 bn — the EEA figure

What member states reported spending out of 2024 ETS auction revenue on indirect cost compensation. That is the basis used in the revenue breakdown on the EU ETS page, where it sits alongside every other spending category on the same footing.

€5.52 bn — the carbon market report figure

Total compensation paid out during 2024 by fifteen member states, for costs incurred in 2023, from whatever source. Larger because it is not limited to ETS revenue and runs on a different year basis.

The two differ by roughly three quarters. Whenever you see a figure for indirect cost compensation, check whether it means money drawn from auction revenue in a given year, or total aid disbursed in that year.

The argument

The case for

Electricity costs are where European manufacturing is genuinely uncompetitive, and the ETS should not penalise firms for electrifying. A carbon price that makes clean electricity expensive relative to gas abroad pushes exactly the wrong way. Industry associations, including the chemicals sector, pressed for wider eligibility on these grounds through 2025.

The case against

Analysts including Sandbag argue that widening the scheme diverts carbon revenue away from decarbonisation into untargeted support, and that compensation paid per tonne of electricity consumed weakens the incentive to consume less of it. In 2024 the payments exceeded what member states spent on social support and just transition combined.

Both are arguments about the same number, and it is now growing at 40% a year while every other leakage instrument is on a declining path.
