Why it exists

Power generators hold no free allowances, so they buy every allowance they need and pass the cost into wholesale electricity prices. An electricity-intensive manufacturer therefore pays for carbon twice: once on its own emissions, and again in its power bill. The second cost is invisible in the ETS accounts and falls hardest on exactly the sectors — aluminium, some chemicals, electric-arc steel — whose competitors abroad face no equivalent.

Member states may reimburse part of that second cost, under Commission State aid guidelines that set who qualifies and how much may be paid. It is therefore not an EU instrument in the way the ETS is: Brussels sets the ceiling, capitals decide whether to pay at all. The formula behind that ceiling is on the How it is calculated tab.

The three leakage instruments, side by side

Free allocation → falling to zero by 2034, replaced one-for-one by CBAM
CBAM → rising to 100% over exactly the same schedule, charged at the border
Indirect cost compensation → no phase-out date, and expanded in December 2025
The first two are locked together by design — the same schedule read from opposite ends. Indirect cost compensation sits entirely outside that interlock. It has no phase-out schedule, no CBAM factor and no equivalent at the border. So as free allocation disappears through 2034, it becomes the main remaining form of carbon leakage protection for electricity-intensive industry, and the one form CBAM does not mirror.

What changed in December 2025

Wider and more generous

The Commission amended the State aid guidelines to add 20 sectors and two subsectors — among them organic chemicals, fertilisers, plastics, flat and hollow glass, ceramic tiles and batteries — and raised the maximum compensable share of indirect costs for the sectors already eligible from 75% to 80%. The newly added ones start at 75%. Member states may ask for further sectors to be added if they show a trade intensity above 20% and an indirect emission intensity above 0.32 kg CO₂ per euro of gross value added. The full list is below.

With conditions attached

Large recipients must now contribute to the green transition, including by investing a share of the aid in projects that reduce electricity system costs. The CO₂ factors and geographic zones used in the compensation formula were updated for 2026–2030.

And after 2030. The ETS review proposal of 17 July 2026, COM(2026) 616, keeps the option open: member states could continue to compensate electro-intensive industry for carbon costs passed through in electricity prices, alongside free allocation that would itself continue to 2040 on conditions. It is a proposal, not law; the benchmarks page draws what it would do to free allocation.

The formula is set in Brussels; the decision to pay is national

Member states design their own schemes, but every scheme has to be notified to the Commission and approved against the ETS State aid Guidelines (2020/C 317/04, supplemented in 2021 and amended in December 2025). The Guidelines fix a single EU-wide formula for the maximum aid an installation may receive for a given year. A member state can pay that maximum, pay less, or not run a scheme at all; several cap their budget and scale every payment down pro rata when claims exceed it. What follows is the ceiling, as it applies to costs incurred from 2026.

Where a product-specific electricity efficiency benchmark exists:
Amax,t = Ai × Ct × Pt−1 × E × AOt
Where it does not (the fall-back):
Amax,t = Ai × Ct × Pt−1 × EF × AECt

Read from the right, the fall-back version is simple: take the electricity the plant actually used, assume only an efficient share of it is eligible, convert it to tonnes of CO₂ with the carbon intensity of the power that sets prices in that region, value those tonnes at the allowance price, and reimburse a fixed share. The product version does the same but measures eligible electricity as output × an efficient electricity use per tonne, so a plant that uses more power than the benchmark is not compensated for the excess.

Pt−1 — allowance price The simple average, over the previous calendar year, of daily closing prices of the EUA one-year forward for December delivery of the aid year. EF — fall-back benchmark Used for eligible products with no Annex II benchmark: a flat share of actual consumption counts as efficient. 0.80 in 2021, reduced by 1.09% a year: 0.757 in 2026, 0.725 in 2030.
AOt, AECt Actual output (tonnes) and actual electricity consumption (MWh) in year t. Measured after the year ends, which is why aid is normally paid in year t+1.

Where the plant is matters as much as what it makes

The CO₂ factor is set per member state or group of member states, so the same plant drawing the same power receives very different maximum aid depending on the zone. With the defaults above, a plant in Germany or Luxembourg (0.73) could receive about 1.7 times what an identical plant in France (0.43) could, and more than twice what one in Austria (0.33) could. Further rules around the formula

A cap on what the firm bears

Where 75–80% does not give enough protection, a member state may limit the indirect costs an undertaking has to bear itself to 1.5% of its gross value added in that year. Aid then covers everything above that, so the effective share can exceed 80%. If used, the limit must apply to every eligible undertaking in the sector.

When it is paid

Aid must be applied for and paid in the year the costs are incurred or the year after. Paid in the same year, it needs an ex-post check and any over-payment is repaid before 1 July of the following year. In practice most schemes pay in year t+1, which is why the trajectory on this page lags the allowance price by a year.

Conditions on the recipient

Beneficiaries must have carried out the energy audit the Energy Efficiency Directive requires and meet one of four conditions: implement the audit recommendations that pay back within three years (or the longer period in Article 10a(1) of the ETS Directive); cover at least 30% of their electricity from carbon-free sources; invest at least 50% of the aid in projects that substantially cut the installation's emissions; or, new since December 2025, invest at least 50% in new or modernised assets that reduce electricity system costs without increasing fossil fuel use.

A transparency trigger

A member state whose schemes cost more than 25% of its auction revenue in a year must publish a report explaining why, including electricity prices for large industrial users. That threshold is what the red bars on the Who pays tab mark.

The trajectory, 2017 to 2025

Compiled from nine successive Commission reports on the functioning of the European carbon market. Each figure is the total aid paid out in that year, for indirect costs incurred the year before.

The driver is the carbon price, not policy generosity

Compensation is calculated from the allowance price of the previous year, so the curve is a lagged echo of the ETS price. The Commission attributes the 2020 doubling and the 2021 jump directly to that: the carbon price used for 2021 compensation was €25.20, against €16.15 for 2019. The single dip, in 2022, followed a year when the price barely moved.

Most schemes have no fixed budget. They reimburse incurred costs up to a ceiling, so the bill rises automatically with the very price signal the ETS exists to create. A successful carbon price and a growing compensation bill are the same phenomenon — which is why this instrument cannot be assumed to shrink on its own.

Why Luxembourg is off the scale. Its compensation is several times its auction revenue because that revenue is tiny: Luxembourg imports around two thirds of its electricity and has no large fossil plants, so it auctions few allowances. France and Belgium are high for a related reason, a largely low-carbon power sector that keeps auction revenue low relative to their electricity-intensive industry. Because the schemes are national and voluntary, a member state with fiscal room can shield its industry and one without cannot — inside a single market the ETS is meant to keep level.

Where the money comes from, and why it is hard to see

Member states report annually, under the Governance Regulation, how they spend ETS auction revenue — 677 named projects across 27 countries for 2024. Searching that dataset for indirect cost compensation returns only five countries and about €1.04 bn, against €5.52 bn actually paid out.

The gap is structural, not an error. Compensation is deducted from auction revenue before the Article 10(3) spending obligation applies — €3.2 bn of 2024 revenue, 13% of the total, was set aside this way — so for most member states it never appears as a reported use of revenue at all. The rest is paid from general budgets. The result is that the EU's own revenue-reporting dataset shows less than a fifth of the money.

There is no category for it either. Of the five countries that do report it as a project, four book it under "15. Other" and Slovenia books it under "14. Administrative expenses". The reporting taxonomy has fourteen substantive categories — energy efficiency, public transport, industry decarbonisation, adaptation and so on — and none of them fits the single largest transfer back to industry.

The symmetry with CBAM scope

Under Article 7 and Annex II of the CBAM Regulation, indirect emissions are excluded from the border charge for precisely the goods whose EU producers receive compensation for indirect costs.

The two columns are mirror images, and deliberately so: the Regulation's recitals say indirect emissions should not initially be counted for goods in respect of which financial measures apply in the Union that compensate for indirect emission costs. Change one and the case for the other changes with it — which is why December 2025's widening is a CBAM scope question, not only an industrial subsidy question.

The argument

The case for

Electricity costs are where European manufacturing is genuinely uncompetitive, and the ETS should not penalise firms for electrifying. A carbon price that makes clean electricity expensive relative to gas abroad pushes exactly the wrong way. Industry associations, including the chemicals sector, pressed for wider eligibility on these grounds through 2025.

The case against

Analysts including Sandbag argue that widening the scheme diverts carbon revenue away from decarbonisation into untargeted support, and that compensation paid per tonne of electricity consumed weakens the incentive to consume less of it. In 2024 the payments exceeded what member states spent on social support and just transition combined.

Two figures, both correct, not comparable

€3.2 bn — the revenue basis

What member states reported financing out of 2024 ETS auction revenue, 13% of the total collected. This is the basis used in the revenue breakdown on the EU ETS page, where it sits alongside every other spending category.

€5.52 bn — the disbursement basis

Total compensation paid out during 2024 by fifteen member states, for costs incurred in 2023, from whatever source. Larger because it is not limited to ETS revenue and runs on a different year basis.

Both figures are for 2024. On the disbursement basis, the Commission's report of 9 November 2026 puts payments made in 2025 at €6.91 bn, for costs incurred in 2024; the matching revenue-basis figure is not yet published.

The two differ by roughly three quarters. Whenever you see a figure for indirect cost compensation, check which basis it uses. The trajectory chart on this site uses the disbursement basis throughout, so its years are comparable with each other.
