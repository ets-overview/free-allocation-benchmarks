Flag-neutral and route-based

Shipping presented the EU with the same problem as aviation: most of a voyage happens outside anyone's territory, and the ship may be registered anywhere. The answer was to ignore the flag entirely and charge according to the route. A vessel flying any flag, owned anywhere, is covered by the same rule if it calls at a European port.

100% — voyages between two EEA ports (Hamburg to Marseille)
100% — emissions inside an EEA port: at berth and manoeuvring
50% — voyages departing an EEA port for outside (Rotterdam to Shanghai)
50% — voyages arriving at an EEA port from outside (Shanghai to Rotterdam)

Charging half of an international voyage is the compromise that made the extension defensible: the EU claims the leg it can plausibly connect to its own territory and leaves the rest. It also means a long-haul container route pays the same absolute amount whichever direction it runs, and that a ship cannot escape the charge by arranging its schedule differently.

Compare this with aviation, where the EU took the opposite approach and simply dropped extra-European flights altogether. Shipping got the half-rule; aviation got an exemption. The difference is largely one of diplomatic timing rather than principle.

Which ships, and which gases

Ships

Since 2024 — cargo and passenger ships of 5 000 gross tonnage and above.
From 2027 — offshore ships of 5 000 GT and above.
Reporting only — since 2025, offshore and general cargo ships between 400 and 5 000 GT must monitor and report, without yet surrendering allowances. That is how the ETS usually expands: measurement first, obligation later.

Gases

CO₂ from 2024.
Methane and nitrous oxide from 2026.
The monitoring regulation has covered all three since 2024, so the data existed for two years before the obligation attached to it. Methane matters more here than the two-year gap suggests: LNG-fuelled ships slip unburned methane through the engine, and until 2026 that escaped the price entirely while counting as a cleaner fuel.

Derogations exist and they are not trivial. Voyages to outermost regions, certain small-island routes, transnational public service contracts, and stops at listed neighbouring container transhipment ports fall partly or wholly outside the surrender obligation. The transhipment carve-out exists to stop cargo being routed through a nearby non-EU port to shed the European leg — a real avoidance route rather than a theoretical one.

Three years to full liability

Shipping was not asked to surrender allowances against all its covered emissions immediately. The obligation phases in, and it is expressed by surrender year rather than emissions year, which is where most of the confusion about this sector comes from.

The first surrender in the sector's history fell due on 30 September 2025, covering 40% of 2024 emissions. The obligation reaches full strength for emissions from 2026, surrendered in 2027.

What a voyage actually pays

The two rules compound. A route's geographic share is multiplied by the phase-in percentage for the year, so the effective share of a voyage's emissions that carries a carbon cost has been well below what either rule suggests on its own. This is the chart that answers "so what is actually charged".

An intra-European voyage went from 40% priced in 2024 to fully priced from 2026. An international voyage went from 20% to 50% — and 50% is where it stays. There is no year in which a Shanghai–Rotterdam sailing pays for all of its emissions.

Why this design was chosen. A phase-in gives an industry with long asset lives and thin margins time to build the cost into charter rates and contracts, and it gave national administrators time to build a registry for thousands of companies that had never touched the ETS. The counter-argument is that a discounted carbon price in the years when fleet renewal decisions are being made is precisely the wrong time to discount it.

The hardest question in the sector: who is "the company"?

For a power station the answer is obvious. For a ship it is not. A vessel may be owned by a single-ship company in one jurisdiction, technically managed by a second firm in another, and commercially operated by a charterer in a third — with the fuel bought by whichever of them the charter contract says. The ETS had to pick one.

The default is the registered owner.
Responsibility may be assumed by the ISM company — the entity holding safety-management responsibility under the International Safety Management Code — where the owner has mandated it in writing.
A bareboat charterer can take it on, but only where it has itself assumed ISM responsibilities for the ship.

Whoever holds it, the obligation is company-level rather than ship-level: the shipping company surrenders allowances for the whole fleet under its responsibility, from a Maritime Operator Holding Account in the Union Registry. And the shipping company always remains the responsible entity for surrendering, whatever commercial arrangement sits behind it.

Who administers it, and who pays in practice

One member state per company

Every shipping company is attributed to a single administering authority: the state where it is registered, or — for a company registered outside the EU — the state where it made most port calls over the preceding four monitoring years. Being absent from the published attribution list is not an exemption; a company that finds itself missing must ask to be added.

The charterer usually bears the cost

Regulatory liability and commercial cost are different things. Where the charterer buys the bunkers and directs the voyage, standard charterparty clauses now pass the allowance cost to them. The ETS deliberately does not regulate this: it fixes a single accountable entity and leaves the industry to allocate the money by contract.

This is the part of the maritime extension that generated most of the implementation work, and none of it is visible in an emissions chart. Thousands of companies, many with no previous European regulatory footprint, had to be identified, attributed to an administrator and given registry accounts before a single allowance could change hands.

Verified emissions, 2024–2025

Two years of data, so this is a starting point rather than a trend. What it does establish is scale: at 90.6 Mt in its first year, maritime immediately became one of the larger activities in the system — roughly a fifth of everything the industrial installations emit, added to the ETS in a single step.

The fall to 80.7 Mt in 2025 should be read cautiously. A first reporting year in a sector with thousands of newly-regulated companies is exactly where you would expect both over- and under-reporting, and the 2025 figure may still be revised. It is far too early to call this a reduction.

This is new coverage, not new emissions. Ships were burning this fuel before 2024; they simply were not counted. When maritime appears in the ETS totals it makes the system's emissions look as though they jumped — a scope change dressed as a trend. The same caveat applies to every chart on this site that shows total ETS emissions across 2023 and 2024.

The carbon price is not the binding constraint

The ETS charges a ship for the carbon it emits. It does nothing to make cleaner fuel exist, and for shipping the alternatives — green methanol, ammonia, e-fuels — are barely in production and cost several times what heavy fuel oil does. A parallel regulation attacks that directly.

What FuelEU Maritime requires

Regulation (EU) 2023/1805, applying from 1 January 2025, sets a declining limit on the greenhouse gas intensity of the energy used on board, measured well-to-wake against a 2020 baseline of 91.16 gCO₂e per megajoule. The reduction is 2% from 2025, tightening in steps to 80% by 2050.

And at berth

From 1 January 2030, container and passenger ships at berth in the major ports designated under the alternative fuels infrastructure rules must connect to onshore power or an equivalent zero-emission technology, rather than running auxiliary engines. This is the measure aimed at port-city air quality as much as at climate.

Note what makes FuelEU different from the ETS: it regulates the intensity of the energy, not the quantity of emissions, and it is well-to-wake, so it counts the emissions from producing the fuel as well as burning it. A ship can comply with the ETS by buying allowances. It cannot comply with FuelEU by buying anything — it has to change what is in the tank.

The global measure that has not arrived

Shipping is the most international industry there is, and the obvious place to price its emissions is the International Maritime Organization rather than one region. The IMO has been working on a Net-Zero Framework — a global fuel-intensity standard combined with a price on emissions above it, which would be the first mandatory greenhouse gas pricing measure applied to an entire global sector.

It has not been adopted. At an extraordinary session of the Marine Environment Protection Committee in October 2025, called specifically to adopt the framework, the meeting instead voted to adjourn for a year — 57 votes to 49, with 21 abstentions — under significant diplomatic pressure against the measure. Negotiations resume in October 2026.

This is a live and genuinely open question, not a settled one. Anything written about the outcome is speculation. What can be said is that the vote was close, the abstentions were numerous, and the delay was procedural rather than a rejection on the merits.

Why the delay matters for the EU scheme

The aviation precedent

The EU has done this before. It priced international aviation, met a diplomatic backlash, and suspended its extra-European scope pending a global scheme — which arrived as CORSIA, an offsetting mechanism far weaker than the ETS, and the suspension is still in force fourteen years later. Shipping is the same situation with the sequence reversed: the EU acted first and has not backed down.

What an IMO deal would mean here

A global measure raises the obvious question of what happens to the regional one. The EU has consistently said it prefers a multilateral solution, which implies some adjustment if a credible one materialises. Whether that means narrowing the ETS scope, crediting IMO payments against it, or leaving both in place is undecided — and undecidable until there is a framework to respond to.

For now a shipping company faces the EU ETS, FuelEU Maritime, the EU monitoring regulation, and the IMO's existing efficiency rules simultaneously — with the possibility of a fifth instrument from 2027 or later.

A first compliance year that went better than expected

For the 2024 compliance year — the sector's first — the Union Registry recorded 3 195 shipping companies meeting the surrender obligation against 121 that did not: a compliance rate of 96.4%.

That is below the 98–100% recorded by sectors regulated since 2005, and above aviation at 91.2%. For a sector in its first ever compliance cycle, covering thousands of companies with no prior ETS experience and many based outside the EU, 96.4% is a stronger result than the extension's critics predicted.

Read it as a floor, not a verdict. The 2024 obligation covered only 40% of emissions, so the financial stake in the first cycle was proportionally small. The real test of enforceability against a non-EU shipping company arrives with the 100% obligation for 2026 emissions — and, in the meantime, nothing here shows what happened to the 121 that did not comply.

Why there is no long time series here

Maritime entered the ETS in 2024, so verified emissions exist for 2024 and 2025 only. Emissions from shipping calling at EU ports were monitored under the MRV Regulation from 2018, but on a different basis and without a surrender obligation, so those figures are not directly comparable with the ETS series and are not shown. Any chart on this site that appears to show maritime emissions rising sharply in 2024 is showing a change in coverage.

On the 2025 figure

The 2025 value of 80.7 Mt comes from the July 2026 data release and reflects reporting as it stood at that point. Compliance reporting for a given year continues to be corrected after first publication, and this is only the sector's second cycle. Treat the 2024–2025 difference as provisional; it is not yet evidence of abatement.
